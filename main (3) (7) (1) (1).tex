\begin{abstract}

Text is the most prevalent form of unstructured data in industry and makes up the vast majority of what enterprises record, analyse and act upon. This data routinely accumulates in enormous volumes with no inherent organisation, far beyond what manual inspection can cover. Clustering is often the crucial first step in analysing this incoming data since it provides the initial insights into a corpus's underlying structure and efficiently organises it into semantically distinct, manageable groups. These methods, however, face a major limitation. Clustering lacks a single correct solution because corpora can inherently be organised across diverse semantic clustering criteria such as domain, intent, or emotion. An ideal corpus clustering, therefore, depends on specific criteria that standard clustering algorithms do not integrate. Evaluation of text clustering faces the same problem, as existing methods are blind to the intended criterion. This thesis asks whether the natural language reasoning ability of Large Language Models, given a one-sentence statement of that criterion, can evaluate and ultimately produce a semantically coherent clustering of an unlabelled corpus. We answer these questions by introducing two bespoke algorithms: the codebook judge and the CLEAR algorithm. The codebook judge evaluates any candidate clustering without the need for ground-truth labels by generating a criterion-conditioned reference clustering and scoring candidates against it. Its scores reach a median Spearman correlation of $+0.87$ with benchmark-label agreement across 19 corpora, after accounting for what geometric label-free metrics measure. The clustering algorithm, \textbf{CLEAR} (\textbf{C}lustering with \textbf{L}LM \textbf{E}valuation \textbf{A}nd \textbf{R}epair), constructs eight candidate clusterings over the encoded documents and uses a Large Language Model to select the strongest candidate and apply post-processing. When compared against 19 benchmarks' best published result, with the encoder and language model matched to those of the record holder, it wins 8 of 19 benchmarks in normalised mutual information and 9 of 19 in clustering accuracy, at a cost of roughly one cent per 100 clustered documents.


\vspace{\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} text clustering, large language models, label-free evaluation, LLM-as-a-judge, clustering criteria, unsupervised learning
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Text Clustering}

Text clustering is the unsupervised organisation of unstructured documents into semantically cohesive groups. Natural language data remains one of the most abundant types of unstructured data in industry and handling massive, unorganised corpora of documents is becoming increasingly vital with the growing reliance on Large Language Models (LLMs) in industry and academia, since LLMs both consume curated text corpora and produce new text at a scale no human team can read, let alone organise. When confronted with a raw corpus of text, the fundamental first question is consistently: ``What is its underlying structure?''. Clustering answers this by partitioning the data into manageable categories in a completely unsupervised way and is therefore essential as a first step in many analytics pipelines. Typical use cases include everything from grouping news articles into stories and organising customer feedback by issue, to analysing large collections of chatbot conversations and clustering legal documents in litigation, and even exposing coordinated misinformation campaigns on social media.

The fundamental difficulty of applying clustering algorithms to text data is that multiple ways to cluster a corpus can make sense semantically. The same collection of documents can be clustered by different factors such as intent, domain, topic and sentiment. These clustering criteria can partition the same corpus in completely different, yet equally semantically valid ways.

\subsection{Classical Methods}

The classical approach to text clustering is a two-step process. First, an encoder is used to transform a sentence or document into a fixed-dimensional vector, where  semantically similar sentences are placed close together in representation space and semantically dissimilar ones far apart. In the second stage, a classical clustering algorithm partitions these vectors into a certain number of groups (or clusters) purely based on the collective geometry of the embedded vectors in their representation space. Both stages are underpinned by substantial fields of research and are explored in detail in \S\ref{sec:representation} and \S\ref{sec:clustering}. The field of computational text representation dates back to 1975 with ``bag-of-words'' methods \citep{salton1975vector}, and has since evolved through learned word vectors to more modern transformer-based methods. The second stage draws on a longer-established field of research that is not specific to text embeddings but can be applied to any kind of multi-dimensional data and encompasses an array of algorithms developed over many years, including k-means, agglomerative linkage, density methods, and graph community detection.

\subsection{Challenges and Motivations}

These two-stage classical methods completely separate semantic representation from the clustering aspect of text clustering. The clustering algorithm only interacts with the vector coordinates. It never reads the documents and has no notion of what the groups it produces semantically represent, so whatever the encoder failed to express cannot be recovered later. This division between the semantic and geometric stages, along with the broader task of unsupervised text clustering, results in three main challenges.

The first challenge is that of underdetermination. Since a corpus can essentially be clustered by many possible clustering criteria (e.g.\ topic, domain, etc.), an encoder will always group that corpus by many different criteria at once. The clustering algorithm then groups the data by whichever criteria happen to dominate the geometry, which is rarely aligned with the user intent. Aside from fine-tuning the encoder itself, there is essentially no way to encode the desired clustering criterion into the geometry.

The second issue is granularity (number of clusters). Most clustering algorithms require knowledge of the number of clusters $k$ in advance. Those that can infer the number of clusters (\S\ref{sec:estimatek}) suffer from the same issue of underdetermination where the intended granularity of the clustering is essentially ambiguous and no semantically superior granularity exists. These clustering algorithms start from the prior assumption that one correct granularity exists. This assumption is essentially invalid when clustering text. The granularity of a corpus is defined by the analyst, and there is no underlying rule that determines this value.

The third challenge with classical unsupervised text clustering is that there is essentially no valid way to evaluate the quality of a clustering in production. The only available substitutes are label-free geometric metrics (\S\ref{sec:labelfree}), which reward clusters that are compact and well-separated in the embedding space. However, it has been well-documented that these break down in the high-dimensional and complex embeddings of text corpora and consistently disagree with human-referenced quality \citep{petukhova2025text,wang2025deep}.

These are the fatal flaws of classical text clustering that the wider field in which this work builds, LLM-in-the-loop clustering, attempts to overcome.

\section{LLM-in-the-Loop Text Clustering}

LLM-in-the-loop text clustering algorithms attempt to bridge this gap between semantic representation and geometric clustering by leveraging the natural language reasoning abilities of LLMs. Use cases for LLMs in these algorithms include improving representation before clustering (\S\ref{sec:before}), guiding assignment during clustering (\S\ref{sec:during}), and refining cluster boundaries after clustering (\S\ref{sec:after}). Some methods are even fully LLM-native, where all geometric clustering aspects of text clustering are removed, and the LLM does all the work.

\subsection{Significance}

Since an LLM receives its instruction in the form of natural language, an LLM-in-the-loop method could allow for a way to insert the clustering criterion into the clustering pipeline and thereby address the issues described above. Interestingly, little to no research has exploited this possibility, although the issue is often addressed \citep{vonluxburg2012science,su2023instructor,zhang2023clusterllm}. No published method takes an arbitrary one-sentence criterion and steers a whole pipeline by it. Most importantly, these methods are evaluated with benchmark labels that a deployed system does not have, so there is no way to evaluate their performance in production by relying on non-geometric signals.

\section{Motivation}

This work was carried out in collaboration with Chattermill, a company whose service analyses customer feedback for large consumer businesses. In this context, the issues with current LLM-in-the-loop methods are even more apparent. A client with a fully unlabelled corpus but a known clustering criterion would have no way of inserting this criterion or even evaluating how well a clustering worked, outside of manual checking. For example, while a client may need customer complaints grouped by specific product issues, a standard algorithm might default to clustering them by general user intent if this dominates the generated geometry. This work treats these shortcomings in the current literature. Evaluation is addressed first to establish label-free metrics that reliably align with human judgment. Granularity is then addressed to determine whether the number of clusters $k$ can be inferred or must be provided as a user-controlled input. A bespoke clustering algorithm is then designed using the results of the first two research questions and is compared to the published state of the art as a clustering method against a variety of benchmarks.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}

\subsection{Research Question 1}

\emph{Given only the criterion underlying a human-generated clustering of a corpus, do the scores of an LLM acting as a judge correlate with human judgement? Is any such correlation more than a restatement of what label-free geometric metrics already measure?}                                                                                                                                                                                         

\vspace{\baselineskip}

On real-world unsupervised datasets, clustering algorithms are not provided with labels to evaluate performance, so there is no easy way to compare one clustering to another. The only available label-free metrics are geometry-based (\S\ref{sec:labelfree}), and mostly reward compact, well-separated geometry, which does not reflect how natural language data is distributed in embedding space. Therefore, these geometry-based metrics have been shown to disagree with human-referenced quality on text (\S\ref{sec:discrepancies}). LLMs have been validated as evaluators of generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}, but have so far been shown to correlate with human judgement only to a limited extent on clustering evaluation tasks \citep{hoyle2025proxann}. Importantly, a simple correlation with human judgement does not, by itself, definitively indicate agreement with human annotators. If the LLM judge's scores turn out to be nothing more than a proxy for geometric compactness and separation, the evaluator will fail in exactly the cases where the label-free geometric metrics already fail. As such, we control for label-free geometric metrics.

\subsection{Research Question 2}

\emph{To what extent can LLMs, given a clustering criterion, infer the granularity of purpose-built human taxonomies from unlabelled data?}

\vspace{\baselineskip}

Most clustering algorithms require prior knowledge of the number of clusters $k$. Classical methods (\S\ref{sec:estimatek}) that infer this value from the data directly make the primary assumption that the dataset has a single granularity to recover in the first place. Natural language data does not necessarily adhere to this assumption, and for a clustering benchmark corpus, the granularity is fully decided by the human annotator together with the chosen clustering criterion. Nearly every method surveyed in \S\ref{sec:llmloop} avoids this issue by taking the ground-truth $k$ as given, which is not available in deployment. The question is whether an LLM, given a certain clustering criterion, is able to align with an annotator's (or many annotators') decision on granularity, given the same criterion.

\subsection{Research Question 3}

\emph{To what extent can an LLM, acting as both partitioner and judge, equipped with prior knowledge of the clustering criterion, and inserted into the clustering pipeline, align unsupervised clustering with the partitions humans would draw?}

\vspace{\baselineskip}

The methods reviewed in \S\ref{sec:llmloop} show that inserting an LLM at one or more stages of the clustering pipeline improves agreement with benchmark labels. However, as noted above, these algorithms still have two main limitations. First, none of the methods attempts to include the clustering criterion (intent, domain, etc.) itself into the pipeline. Second, every method relies on the same baseline geometric clustering of the corpus. This thesis attempts to leverage language reasoning not only to improve the baseline clustering, but also to judge which clustering to use in the first place.

\subsection{Research Question 4}

\emph{Can a single, general clustering algorithm, equipped with a stated criterion and a label-free LLM judge, match or exceed published LLM-in-the-loop methods across the published benchmark suite when the encoder and language model are held equal?}

\vspace{\baselineskip}

For each benchmark discussed in this work, a single algorithm scores highest on each evaluation metric. These algorithms are considered the state of the art but, importantly, can only be compared against on a same-document, same-encoder, and same-LLM basis. As such, we use a different configuration of the final algorithm for each comparison.

\section{A Brief History of Text Representation}\label{sec:representation}

\subsection{Sparse Features and Static Word Vectors}

The earliest proposed method for computational text representation \citep{salton1975vector} was a ``bag-of-words'' approach in which words in a sentence or paragraph are represented as sparse points in a vocabulary-indexed vector space, where the dimensionality of the representation space equals a chosen vocabulary length. Pointwise weighting schemes such as Term Frequency--Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) \citep{salton1988term} later refined this by weighting sparse points by frequency in the document or sentence. Although these methods made retrieval and clustering computationally feasible, they quantified text similarity only through exact overlap of used words. In these representation spaces, semantically similar sentences phrased differently still appear completely dissimilar, making semantic clustering infeasible.

The first attempt to address this semantic mismatch was Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)  \citep{deerwester1990lsa}, which applies matrix factorisation (truncated singular value decomposition) to the word--document count matrix to capture co-occurrence patterns that link words used in similar contexts. By drastically reducing the available dimensions, the algorithm forces words that frequently share the same surrounding vocabulary into grouped slots. Probabilistic successors such as latent Dirichlet allocation later extended this idea \citep{blei2003lda}.

A more significant leap in capturing semantic meaning came later with learned, dense word vectors. Word2vec \citep{mikolov2013efficient,mikolov2013distributed} revealed that a shallow model trained to predict which words appear near which others in a sentence yields vectors whose underlying geometry in representation space encodes semantic regularity. This methodology was later expanded by algorithms such as GloVe \citep{pennington2014glove}, which incorporates global co-occurrence statistics, and fastText \citep{bojanowski2017fasttext}, which extends to sub-word units to include rare and misspelt words. For document clustering purposes, the vectors to cluster are taken as the average of all the word vectors in the document.

These approaches came with two significant limitations, however. First, averaging word vectors in a document loses all information about word order and document composition. Second, and most importantly, the mapping from word or subword to its corresponding vector in the representation space is static. This prevents the vector from adapting to the word's surrounding context. These rather significant limitations partly motivated the development of the next generation of text-representation algorithms that are, at the time of writing, still considered state-of-the-art methods: Transformer-Based Sentence Encoders.

\subsection{Transformer-Based Sentence Encoders}\label{sec:transenc}

Introduced by Google AI in 2018, the BERT  encoder \citep{devlin2019bert} uses bi-directional self-attention to produce a token representation that is conditioned on all other tokens in the sentence (forward and backwards) in order to fully incorporate the context in which the token sits into its vector representation. Although transformative, BERT suffered from highly anisotropic token vector representations \citep{ethayarajh2019contextual}. The embedded vectors only occupy a very narrow cone in representation space, making them difficult to discriminate and thus cluster by cosine distance. Some early workarounds included post-hoc corrections to the representation space itself \citep{li2020sentence,su2021whitening}. However, a more substantial remedy to the issue of anisotropy came in training explicitly for sentence-level similarity in which individual tokens are replaced with entire token sequences so that the final representation places semantically similar sentences close together. These sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sbert} encoders are trained by fine-tuning BERT-level encoders through sentence-pair supervision (labelled example pairs of semantically similar sentences). Subsequently, researchers expanded the fine-tuning data through a process known as contrastive learning, in which semantically similar sentence pairs (question-answer pairs, perturbed versions of sentences, paraphrasing, etc.) are pulled together, and semantically unrelated pairs are ``pulled apart'' in representation space. SimCSE  \citep{gao2021simcse} showed that a remarkably simple version of this methodology already yields strong sentence embeddings. Other sentence embedding models based on similar contrastive fine-tuning approaches, such as E5 \citep{wang2022e5} and GTE \citep{li2023gte}, also showed promising early performance. In this work, the term ``encoder'' refers to these types of sentence embedders. These encoders typically map to a vector space of anywhere between 384 and 1{,}024 dimensions and, at the time of writing, form the representational base of what most modern text-clustering pipelines are built on, with a few exceptions for encoder-free methods which are discussed in \S\ref{sec:llmnative}.

\subsection{Instruction-Tuned Encoders}

Another relatively recent improvement to sentence encoders which are particularly relevant to semantic clustering are instruction-tuned encoders. The encoders discussed in \S\ref{sec:transenc} all have a fixed, general-purpose notion of semantic similarity. This general notion is not task-specific and is somehow learned from the general aggregate of its training data. An instruction-tuned encoder, instead, accepts a natural-language description of the task together with its usual input and conditions its geometry on this criterion without further fine-tuning. Instruction-tuned encoders such as INSTRUCTOR \citep{su2023instructor} allow the user to prepend an instruction such as ``Represent the sentence for topic clustering: ''. During its initial multi-task training phase, the network learned to associate diverse natural language instructions with their optimal target vector spaces. Because of this extensive prior training, a single frozen model (weights fixed after training) produces embeddings dynamically adapted to the stated criterion.

Combined with a clustering algorithm, these instruction-tuned encoders enable domain-specific clustering of a corpus. For example, given a corpus of customer feedback reports for a mobile app, an instruction-tuned embedder could be given a criterion for which aspect of customer feedback the user wants the corpus clustered. Instructed to represent the feedback reports for clustering by topic rather than sentiment, the encoder should place two reports about the billing system close together even if one praises the interface and the other complains about crashes. The instruction-tuned embedder can thus shift which similarities the geometry emphasises and thus allow for easier criterion-conditioned clustering down the line.

While instruction-tuning represents a significant recent advancement in the context of this thesis, the broader field of sentence encoders is vast and rapidly evolving. The field includes a wide array of alternative models and architectures, including LLM-based embedders \citep{behnamghader2024llm2vec,muennighoff2025gritlm,lee2025nvembed} and distilled encoders \citep{zhang2024stella}.

\subsection{The MTEB Benchmark}\label{sec:mteb}

The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) consolidates evaluations of many different sentence encoders across various task types, including clustering. For clustering evaluation, candidate encoders are evaluated by running the same simple clustering algorithm (mini-batch k-means; \S\ref{sec:algorithms}) on fixed corpora and measuring agreement with reference human-generated clusterings \citep{muennighoff2023mteb}, recently extended multilingually \citep{enevoldsen2025mmteb}.

\section{Classical Text Clustering}\label{sec:clustering}

Classical (non-LLM) text clustering techniques rely on a well designed encoder to map a corpus to an $n$-dimensional embedding space, in addition to classical geometry-based clustering algorithms. These can vary in complexity and fall into four broad families: centroid-based, hierarchical, density-based and graph-based methods \citep{xie2016dec,zhang2021sccl}.

\subsection{Clustering Algorithms}\label{sec:algorithms}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Centroid methods} assign each datapoint to a cluster defined by a central point, or centroid, in the embedding space. The most widely used example is k-means \citep{macqueen1967some,lloyd1982least}, which locally minimises within-cluster variance by iteratively assigning points to their nearest centroid and updating each centroid to the mean of its assigned data points. As results are initialisation-dependent, k-means is usually run several times, and its results averaged. Another method of mitigating the dependence on random initialisation is the k-means++ algorithm \citep{arthur2007kmeanspp}. It starts by choosing a single datapoint randomly and iteratively adds new centroids by selecting from the remaining data points with a probability proportional to their squared distance from the nearest existing centroid, until all $k$ initial centres are chosen. Careful seeding \citep{arthur2007kmeanspp} and mini-batch updates \citep{sculley2010webscale} make k-means the default at scale. As it minimises within cluster variance, k-means algorithms have an inherent bias towards spherical clusterings. One alternative algorithm that mitigates this bias is Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs). These models allow for ellipsoidal (Gaussian) cluster shapes and enable soft assignment by representing the underlying data points as weighted sums of multivariate Gaussian distributions. GMMs are fitted through Expectation-Maximisation \citep{dempster1977em}.
\item \emph{Hierarchy methods} build a tree of merges starting from the bottom where each data point is considered its own cluster and clusters are iteratively merged based on a chosen linkage rule. Ward's linkage, which merges the pair of clusters that least increases within-cluster variance, is the standard choice for embeddings \citep{ward1963hierarchical}. The tree is then cut at a chosen level to yield a desired number of clusters.
\item \emph{Density-based methods} define clusters as high-density regions where points are only assigned to their nearest cluster if they lie within a certain density threshold and unassigned points in low-density regions are simply treated as noise or outliers. These methods allow for arbitrarily shaped clusters. The most widely used examples are DBSCAN \citep{ester1996dbscan} and its hierarchical successor HDBSCAN \citep{campello2013hdbscan}.
\item \emph{Graph methods} represent the data as connected nodes in which each point is connected to its nearest neighbours (usually defined as points within some cosine similarity threshold or $k$-nearest neighbours) as a graph network with edge weights. This graph is then partitioned into well connected communities (clusters) where inter-community edges are dense and intra-community edges are mostly sparse. This partition is usually achieved spectrally whereby the weakest edges between regions are severed \citep{ng2002spectral,vonluxburg2007tutorial}. Another common approach is through bottom-up community detection, where communities are built agglomeratively by starting at the level of individual nodes and progressively merging them into larger structures based on some condition. The most common approach here is Leiden clustering \citep{traag2019leiden}, where a partition is chosen in which modularity is maximised. Modularity is the metric that defines how much more edge weight falls inside communities than would be expected if the same nodes were connected at random. Leiden clustering builds upon the previously developed Louvain clustering by mathematically guaranteeing that all resulting communities remain internally connected, thereby preventing arbitrarily disconnected sparse communities, which Louvain is known to produce \citep{blondel2008louvain}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Geometric Assumptions of Clustering Algorithms on Text Embeddings}\label{sec:assumptions}

Each family of clustering algorithms carries with it some inductive bias through assumptions of the underlying data distribution. The k-means objective assumes compact, roughly spherical, comparably sized clusters. Ward's linkage in hierarchical clustering makes similar variance-based assumptions, where clusters are merged to minimise the increase in within-cluster variance which implicitly favours compact and similarly sized clusters. Gaussian mixtures somewhat relax the cluster shape to ellipsoids, whereas density-based methods assume clusters separated by low-density regions. These assumptions can be made for many different data types, and they thus prove very useful by themselves in many relatively low-dimensional applications such as image segmentation, bioinformatics, and customer profiling.

However, when it comes to clustering text representations in relatively high-dimensional feature space, some of these methods break down. In spaces of hundreds of dimensions, distances become less discriminative. The contrast between a point's nearest and furthest neighbours shrinks (\citealp{beyer1999nearest,aggarwal2001surprising}), and some points may therefore recur among many other points' nearest neighbours (\emph{hubness}) which can distort the underlying neighbourhood structure \citep{radovanovic2010hubs}. While many modern embedder frameworks, such as sentence-BERT models, partly resolve the issue of extreme anisotropy of embedded text data through contrastive training, this data still often exhibits complex topologies in embedding space rather than compact, easily separable regions. Consequently, compactness-seeking objectives will often incorrectly fragment or merge them. Early comparative studies on documents found different algorithm families trading places in clustering quality depending on the specific characteristics of the corpus itself \citep{steinbach2000comparison}. There is therefore a practical ``no free lunch'' theorem for text clustering whereby no single one of these classical geometric objectives is appropriate and works well across all types of corpora.

A final major assumption of these algorithms is that the correct cluster structure is inherently encoded in the data's geometry. For the semantic clustering of text, this assumption fails. Returning to the previous example, a corpus of customer feedback can be validly organised by multiple criteria, such as intent, domain, sentiment, or phrasing. A single embedding space captures all these aspects at varying strengths, but a purely geometric algorithm will simply recover whichever structure happens to dominate the space. In this regime, classical clustering algorithms have no input on which aspect of the corpus the clustering is based. Consequently, evaluating a purely geometric algorithm against a single human-annotated benchmark is fundamentally flawed, because the benchmark reflects only one of several valid human criteria. As \citet{vonluxburg2012science} argue, clustering objectives cannot be meaningfully evaluated apart from their intended use.

To change the geometry of the encoded text data towards a specific clustering criterion, one option is to fine-tune the embedder itself, such as through the use of instruction-tuned models. This exact strategy of fine-tuning an embedder in order to increase downstream classical clustering performance is what underpins an active field of research. Progress across this field is primarily tracked by the MTEB benchmark discussed in \S\ref{sec:mteb}, as a standard leader board for evaluation of how well different encoders capture a given intended semantic clustering criterion. Another strategy to overcome this anisotropy is to enhance the clustering algorithm itself by leveraging the ever-increasing language understanding capability of LLMs in the clustering loop (\S\ref{sec:llmloop}).

\subsection{Estimating the Number of Clusters}\label{sec:estimatek}

The clustering algorithms in the previous section can be divided into two main categories: oracle-$k$ and non-oracle-$k$ methods. Oracle-$k$ methods, such as k-means, GMMs, hierarchical methods, and spectral clustering, require prior knowledge of the number of clusters in the distribution ($k$). Methods such as DBSCAN/HDBSCAN and Leiden/Louvain clustering can automatically infer this value by applying spatial density thresholds or by maximising the modularity score, respectively.

In addition to these methods, many algorithms have been proposed to infer the intrinsic granularity of datasets. A common approach to $k$-inference is a bottom-up ``elbow'' approach where as $k$ increases you stop at the point (the ``elbow'') where adding more clusters stops significantly reducing cluster spread and take this as your granularity \citep{thorndike1953who}. Another approach is simply to choose the granularity that maximises the silhouette score (\S\ref{sec:labelfree}) \citep{rousseeuw1987silhouettes}; related criteria include the gap statistic \citep{tibshirani2001gap} and X-means \citep{pelleg2000xmeans}. Bayesian nonparametric mixture models treat the number of components as unknown and infer it through Bayesian inference with a nonparametric prior over mixture components \citep{antoniak1974mixtures,rasmussen2000infinite}. Finally, stability-based methods infer the $k$ whose clusterings are most reproducible across resamples of the data \citep{benhur2002stability,vonluxburg2010stability}.

These granularity estimation methods all rely on the core assumption that one true intrinsic granularity exists for any given dataset. When it comes to text, this assumption is somewhat relaxed. Text corpora can often be clustered at multiple granularities at once, depending on the clustering criterion. For example, a corpus can have a relatively high granularity when clustered for domain, but only have a small amount of clusters that correspond to intent. For benchmark corpora with labelled cluster assignments, the target value of $k$ is determined by the annotation decisions of the researchers who made the benchmark rather than by an intrinsic property of the embedding space, so algorithms designed to infer $k$ are effectively estimating that choice rather than recovering a uniquely correct number of clusters. Consequently, many modern text clustering algorithms require prior knowledge of $k$ (oracle-$k$), although exceptions exist. This tension is exactly what research question 2 investigates.

\section{Evaluation of Clusterings}\label{sec:evaluation}

Metrics for evaluating the quality of the clustering of a given dataset can be divided into two main categories: label-free and label-based, where label-free metrics allow us to evaluate purely on geometric grounds, while label-based metrics can only be used in addition to a ground-truth clustering that is usually generated by researchers, which in the case of this thesis comes in the form of benchmark datasets (\S\ref{sec:benchmarks}).

\subsection{Label-Free Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:labelfree}

There are three main label-free evaluation metrics, with comparative studies finding no one index superior across diverse data conditions \citep{arbelaitz2013extensive}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The silhouette score} is an effective metric to score how well separated and cohesive the computed clusters are. It compares each point's mean intra-cluster distance to its mean nearest-cluster distance averaged over all points in the dataset. This yields a score near $+1$ when clusters are compact and well separated, near $0$ when they overlap, and negative when points are misassigned \citep{rousseeuw1987silhouettes}.
\item \emph{The Cali\'nski--Harabasz (CH) index} computes the ratio of between-cluster dispersion to within-cluster dispersion, normalised by degrees of freedom for $N$ data points and $k$ clusters. This metric is high and unbounded positive when clusters are dense and well-isolated, and approaches zero when clusters are diffuse and overlapping \citep{calinski1974dendrite}.
\item \emph{The Davies--Bouldin index} evaluates each cluster exclusively against its most overlapping neighbour. It calculates the maximum ratio of combined within-cluster scatter to centroid separation for every cluster and then averages these worst-case scores. Scores approach 0 for compact and widely separated clusters and increase as clusters become diffuse or overlapping \citep{davies1979cluster}. It is therefore a measure of worst-case structural overlap.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Label-Based Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:labelbased}

\emph{The Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)} takes all possible pairs of documents in a corpus (or a random subset for computational reasons) and generates the rate at which the clustering and the ground-truth labels agree on each pair (same group in both, or different in both). This is then corrected for the agreement expected by chance so that it takes values in $[-1, 1]$, with an expected value of 0 if clusters were assigned randomly \citep{hubert1985comparing}.

Mutual Information (MI) $I(U;V)$ measures the mutual dependence between two random variables $U$ and $V$. Calculating it tells us how much knowing the outcome of one reduces uncertainty about the other. In the context of clustering, it measures the reduction in uncertainty about the ground truth given the predicted clusters (or vice versa). \emph{Normalised Mutual Information (NMI)} normalises the MI by the average of the entropies of the ground-truth label $H(U)$ and predicted class $H(V)$. This bounds the score between 0 and 1, normalising for the inherent uncertainty in both partitions:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NMI}(U,V) = \frac{I(U;V)}{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(H(U)+H(V)\right)}
\end{equation}
When comparing clusterings of different sizes, even completely random assignments will yield a score greater than zero by pure chance. Because this effect increases as the number of clusters grows, NMI artificially increases for more fine-grained clusterings. \emph{Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI)} offsets NMI's bias towards fine grained clustering by subtracting the expected mutual information $\mathbb{E}[I]$ if you were to randomly assign all data points at the same granularity of your chosen clustering \citep{vinh2010information}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AMI}(U,V) = \frac{I(U;V) - \mathbb{E}[I]}{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(H(U)+H(V)\right) - \mathbb{E}[I]}
\end{equation}
AMI is therefore the better choice when comparing clustering at different granularities. A related entropy-based alternative is the V-measure \citep{rosenberg2007vmeasure}.

\emph{Accuracy (ACC)} finds the best one-to-one matching between predicted clusters and true classes, then calculates the rate of correct assignments:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ACC} = \max_{\sigma} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\!\left[ \sigma(c_i) = t_i \right]
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the total number of data points, $c_i$ is the predicted cluster assignment for the $i$-th point, $t_i$ is its true class label, $\sigma$ is a permutation mapping cluster labels to true class labels. This optimal mapping $\sigma$ is in practice calculated with the compute-efficient Hungarian algorithm \citep{kuhn1955hungarian}. One caveat here is that, since matchings are one-to-one, accuracy heavily penalises a clustering that splits one ground-truth class into two clusters, even if the split is semantically reasonable.

\subsection{Discrepancies Between Label-Free and Label-Based Evaluation on Text}\label{sec:discrepancies}

On text embeddings, label-free and label-based evaluation metrics are known to often disagree. A recent comparison of text embeddings found that configurations achieving high label-based metrics could also receive low silhouette and Cali\'nski--Harabasz index scores which indicates good agreement with the ground-truth labels despite relatively low cluster compactness and separation in the embedding space \citep{petukhova2025text}. Similarly, a statistical analysis of label-free evaluations of datasets in learned embedding spaces concluded that standard label-free metrics are unreliable in the text-clustering regime \citep{wang2025deep}. Similar evidence appears in single-cell genomics, where silhouette-based evaluation of learned embeddings is often systematically misleading \citep{rautenstrauch2025shortcomings}. The main consensus is that label-free metrics are not guaranteed to align with human judgement because semantically consistent clusterings often form complex, non-compact geometries in embedding space. This thesis investigates to what capacity the semantic reasoning ability of LLMs can be leveraged to produce label-free evaluations that agree with human judgement in order to provide a reliable label-free metric in the natural language processing (NLP) clustering domain.

\section{LLM-in-the-Loop Text Clustering}\label{sec:llmloop}

Since 2023, a growing field of research known as LLM-in-the-loop clustering has emerged. Proposed methodologies attempt to harness the natural language reasoning capabilities of LLMs inserted into bespoke clustering algorithms in order to address the gap between semantic clustering and geometric representation discussed in the previous section. Because LLM inference is more computationally expensive than standard distance calculations but cheaper than human annotation, these systems are often designed to optimise where they apply LLMs. \citet{viswanathan2024fewshot} categorise LLM-in-the-loop clustering algorithms into three stages: before clustering (improving representations), during clustering (constraining assignments), and after clustering (correcting results) and current research largely implements one or more of these strategies. One additional approach discussed in this thesis are fully LLM-native methods that do not include any classical clustering or encoding methods and instead rely solely on the LLM.

The methods discussed below were chosen on three main criteria from the literature. First, the methods must have results published in peer-reviewed papers. Second, the language model must be used inside the clustering pipeline. Finally, the paper must report results on the benchmarks of \S\ref{sec:benchmarks}, so that a like-for-like comparison is possible. Additionally, methods that see any amount of ground-truth labels, such as semi-supervised algorithms, are also excluded. Adjacent lines of work fall outside these criteria and are not compared: semi-supervised generalised category discovery \citep{vaze2022gcd,an2024generalized,liang2024alup}, classification-style LLM clustering with seed labels \citep{huang2025tcc}, LLM-based taxonomy generation and corpus-mining systems \citep{wang2023goalex,wan2024tntllm,tamkin2024clio}, human-alignment studies of LLM clustering \citep{miller2025human,hong2025dialin}, and agentic or reinforcement-based clustering \citep{xu2025clusterfusion,lowe2026agentic,qing2026clusterr1}. Figure~\ref{fig:roster-timeline} shows an overview of the 11 LLM-in-the-loop algorithms compared in this thesis and Table~\ref{tab:roster} (Appendix~\ref{app:litstat}) provides a detailed breakdown of each method's headline configuration, encoder backbone, and language model.

\subsection{Improving the Representation Before Clustering}\label{sec:before}

Pre-clustering methods implement LLMs to augment document representations before applying any kind of clustering algorithm in order to add more semantic information to the document representations to increase the semantic signal to noise ratio.
\begin{itemize}
\item Few-Shot Clustering \citep{viswanathan2024fewshot} augments document representations with semantically dense information by concatenating the original vectors with encoded LLM-generated keyphrases. To achieve this, it prompts an LLM to generate a given amount of keyphrases for each document. These keyphrases are then encoded using the same encoder as the original text, and the resulting keyphrase vectors are concatenated to the corresponding original representation vectors, doubling the dimensionality of the embedding space.
\item The IDAS algorithm \citep{deraedt2023idas} replaces the original document representation entirely with the vector of an LLM-generated summary. Expanding on the previous idea, it uses an LLM to generate a complete contrastive summary of the document and then represents the document solely by the vector representation of that summary.
\item Similarly, the LUMI algorithm \citep{lin2025lumi} enhances representations by averaging the original text vectors with embedded LLM-generated pseudo-labels. It achieves this by using multiple prompts and/or models to generate pseudo-labels for each document, which are then embedded and averaged with the original text vectors.
\item The SPILL clustering algorithm \citep{lin2025spill} pulls documents of the same intent closer together by representing each document as an average of its own embedding and LLM-verified same-intent neighbours. Designed specifically for clustering by intent rather than by any general notion of category or meaning, SPILL first generates a candidate pool for a given target document containing its nearest neighbours in embedding space. For each neighbour in the pool, an LLM is prompted to judge whether that candidate expresses the same general intent as the target. The encoded vectors of the accepted candidates are averaged together with the target's own embedding, so that each document is represented by a small, LLM-verified group of same-intent documents rather than by its own text alone.
\item The Bag-of-Texts approach \citep{lin2025bagoftexts} attempts to completely eliminate dependence on the original geometry by replacing the notion of distance in embedding space with an LLM-judged similarity to a set of representative anchors. This is done by first picking $1{,}024$ representative documents (anchors) as the medoids of a hierarchical clustering at $k = 1{,}024$. These documents are then treated the same way bag-of-words algorithms treat a vocabulary, where each document represents a one-hot-encoded vector in a 1{,}024-dimensional vector space. For each remaining document, an LLM is then shown the document together with a shortlist of the anchors most similar to it and is asked: which of these, if any, express the same thing? The ticks on the checklist become the document's new representation in 1{,}024-dimensional space. For example, a complaint about app crashes might tick a given set of anchors whereas another similar app crash complaint that is phrased differently will usually also tick the same set of anchors. As such, the two documents receive nearly identical representations even though they might have very different wording. With this mechanism, distance in this new space describes how much an LLM judged the two documents to belong together. Regular clustering then recovers those groups that have been judged to be similar.
\item ClusterLLM \citep{zhang2023clusterllm} fine-tunes the sentence encoder itself instead of augmenting the data representations directly, using LLM judgments on difficult similarity triplets. Specifically, an LLM is used to generate answers to triplet questions of the form ``which of B or C is more similar to A?''. For example, given the anchor A = ``the app keeps crashing'' and the candidates B = ``app freezes right after start-up'' and C = ``my delivery arrived two days late'', the LLM answers B. The encoder is then fine-tuned so that its distances agree with the answer. As such, A is ``pulled'' towards B and away from C in vector space. These triplets are sampled close to the boundaries between clusters, where the current representation of the corpus is most uncertain about cluster assignment. As such, only the difficult examples are adjusted. This procedure is also repeated over several rounds with the two different embedders (INSTRUCTOR and E5), so there are three main variants of the algorithm: ClusterLLM-I (INSTRUCTOR, one round), ClusterLLM-I-iter (INSTRUCTOR, iterated), and ClusterLLM-E-iter (E5, iterated).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Guiding Assignment During Clustering}\label{sec:during}

The second approach is to integrate the LLM directly into the clustering objective of classical clustering algorithms to change them into constrained optimisation problems \citep{wagstaff2001constrained}. The constraints come in the form of \emph{must-link} constraints (these two documents belong in the same cluster) and \emph{cannot-link} constraints (they do not) generated by the LLM, which the algorithm is rewarded for satisfying.
\begin{itemize}
\item Few-Shot Clustering uses an LLM to generate must-link and cannot-link constraints that penalize a k-means objective. It first selects document pairs from the corpus by the Explore--Consolidate strategy \citep{basu2004active}. These pairs are then passed to an LLM through a prompt requesting a must-link or cannot-link label per pair. The generated constraints are passed to pairwise-constrained k-means (PCKMeans), which minimises
\[
\mathcal{J} \;=\; \sum_{x_i \in \mathcal{X}} \lVert x_i - \mu_{l_i} \rVert^2 \;+\; \sum_{(x_i, x_j) \in \mathcal{M}} w \,\mathbf{1}\!\left[ l_i \neq l_j \right] \;+\; \sum_{(x_i, x_j) \in \mathcal{C}} \bar{w} \,\mathbf{1}\!\left[ l_i = l_j \right],
\]
where $\mathcal{X}$ is the embedded corpus, $l_i$ is the cluster assigned to document $x_i$, and $\mu_{l_i}$ its centroid. The first term is the ordinary k-means objective (within-cluster variance). The second adds a penalty $w$ for every must-link pair in $\mathcal{M}$ that is split across clusters. The third is a penalty $\bar{w}$ for every cannot-link pair in $\mathcal{C}$ placed in the same cluster. As such, the judgement of the LLM is traded off against the classical embedding distances with the trade-off rate set by the penalty weights $w$ and $\bar{w}$ \citep{viswanathan2024fewshot}.
\item The Cequel algorithm \citep{wang2025cequel} explicitly trades off clustering quality with LLM cost by strategically targeting the most ambiguous relations for constraint generation. It works similarly to Few-Shot clustering but optimises for LLM-call cost by starting with a fixed maximum number of queries and adjusted spending strategies. Cequel begins by generating a similarity graph over the corpus where each document vector is a node and every edge weighted between nodes is their embedding cosine similarity. Two spending strategies then exist. EdgeLLM starts by scoring each edge by its spanning edge centrality (the measure that is high for edges bridging otherwise weakly connected regions and low for edges inside dense neighbourhoods). It then greedily chooses a given amount of pairs with the highest centrality as these represent the most ambiguous pairs of documents. TriangleLLM selects triangles of three documents in the same greedy way and gets the relation between every two of them in a single LLM call, so one query yields three pairwise constraints rather than one. These labelled must-link or cannot-link pairs are then used in a pairwise constrained k-means algorithm as before.
\item k-LLMmeans \citep{diazrodriguez2026kllmmeans} modifies the standard k-means algorithm by replacing geometrically calculated centroids with embedded LLM-generated cluster summaries. It achieves this by inserting an LLM into the recursive loop. Instead of recursively recalculating the centroids as the mean of cluster assigned points, k-LLMmeans samples a few documents from the cluster and prompts an LLM to write a short summary of the cluster's documents. This summary is then embedded and taken as the updated centroid. With this adjustment, cluster centres are chosen semantically rather than geometrically, remain human-readable, and are less sensitive to outlying members.
\item NILC extends k-LLMmeans by guiding assignment with both semantic and geometric centroids simultaneously, and by having the LLM rewrite ambiguous documents. First, LLM descriptions are taken alongside regular Euclidean distance centroids as dual centroids so that point assignment in the next round is guided by both. Second, it rewrites a small set of ambiguous documents. After each clustering round, every document's distances to the $k$ centroids are converted through a softmax into an assignment distribution, and the documents with the highest entropies are selected as ambiguous samples. The LLM is then shown each ambiguous document along with its centroid summary and is prompted to rewrite it more explicitly in that context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Refining Clusters After Clustering}\label{sec:after}

Post-clustering refinements use LLMs to reassign ambiguous points that lie far from their cluster centroids in embedding space after the main clustering has been performed.
\begin{itemize}
\item In addition to pre-clustering improvements, Few-Shot Clustering \citep{viswanathan2024fewshot} also reclassifies points after clustering. This is done by identifying the $n$ points with the smallest margin between their distances to the nearest and second-nearest centroids. For each of these points, an LLM is then prompted to decide which of the two clusters it belongs to by showing it representative documents (points close to the centroid) from each. Points are finally reassigned based on the LLM's decision.
\item LLMEdgeRefine \citep{feng2024llmedgerefine} targets the same low-confidence points in a two-stage process starting from a classical k-means clustering of the embedded documents. In the first stage, the points furthest away from their centroid are identified and their corresponding text documents are combined into one larger document. This concatenation of documents is then embedded as one ``super point'' and it is used to replace the original points. All the data is then reclustered agglomeratively. This stage simultaneously stops outliers from dragging their cluster's centroid towards the edge and lets a group of stray points migrate together. The LLM is then used in the second stage where over a few iterations, the points now furthest from each refined centroid are individually reassessed as before.
\end{itemize}
Post-clustering refinement methods are useful because can easily be combined with every other family in this section. However, one unexplored risk does exist when implementing these types of methods. Iterated LLM-guided correction optimises agreement with the LLM's own notion of the taxonomy, which may drift from the analyst's (in this work the researchers that generated a given benchmark dataset). Section~\ref{sec:judgebiases} discusses an analogous, better-documented bias in LLM evaluation. This question is also empirically investigated in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}.

\subsection{LLM-Native Clustering}\label{sec:llmnative}

LLM-Native clustering completely removes the geometric aspect and has the LLM itself perform the entire clustering. The main issue with a fully LLM-native approach is that each LLM call is stateless where each call begins with no recollection of the previous one so a model prompted to group a corpus batch by batch (which is necessary for large corpora due to a limited context window) cannot keep its groups consistent. LLM-MemCluster \citep{zhu2025memcluster} solves this by supplying the LLM explicitly with a dynamic memory (the list of clusters discovered so far with their labels) at each prompt. The LLM can then assign documents to existing clusters, open new clusters for genuinely distinct topics, and merge or rename existing ones and the updated dynamic memory is then carried into the next LLM call. Two main problems arise with this approach, however. First, each document needs to be passed through an LLM, these methods get significantly costly for large corpora, which is something encoder-based pipelines avoid by leveraging classical clustering as a base. Second, with no encoder in the loop, representational quality and LLM reasoning quality can not be disentangled from each other, and thus no encoder-matched comparison can be made to other clustering techniques.

\section{Benchmarks for Text Clustering}\label{sec:benchmarks}

\subsection{The Standard Evaluation Suite}\label{sec:suite}

Because unlabelled corpora by themselves do not allow for quantitative evaluation outside of label-free evaluation metrics, which have been shown to break down in the regime of text clustering (\S\ref{sec:discrepancies}), a line of benchmarks has been proposed as a standard evaluation suite in the literature. These clustering benchmarks mostly repurpose classification datasets, where the method sees only the input and uses the class labels only afterwards as cluster labels. A fairly stable and widely used suite has emerged of mostly intent, domain and topic benchmarks. Every clustering algorithm proposed in the field's literature is evaluated on a specific subset of these benchmarks. This thesis compares against 19 of these benchmarks, namely every corpus in the suite for which a peer-reviewed clustering result has been published.

Six of them are intent benchmarks: Banking77 \citep{casanueva2020banking77}, CLINC(I) \citep{larson2019clinc}, MTOP(I) \citep{li2021mtop}, MASSIVE(I) \citep{fitzgerald2023massive}, SNIPS \citep{coucke2018snips} and M-CID \citep{arora2020mcid} with the last covering COVID-19 queries. Three further benchmarks reuse the CLINC, MTOP and MASSIVE corpora but label them by domain instead of intent, giving CLINC(D), MTOP(D) and MASSIVE(D). The documents are identical for MTOP and MASSIVE, while CLINC(D) drops the 1{,}000 out-of-scope utterances that carry no domain label. Because only the labels differ on the shared documents, these three pairs of benchmarks show that a corpus has no single correct clustering independent of the specific clustering criterion, as discussed in \S\ref{sec:assumptions}. Six are topic benchmarks: BBC News \citep{greene2006bbc}, Tweet89 \citep{yin2016model} and StackOverflow \citep{xu2017selftaught}, together with ArXiv-S2S, Reddit-S2S and StackEx-S2S, three corpora re-split for clustering by \citet{zhang2023clusterllm}. The remaining four ask for organisations that are not topical at all: DBPedia \citep{zhang2015character} and FewNerd \citep{ding2021fewnerd} group by entity type, FewRel \citep{han2018fewrel} by the relation between two entities, and FewEvent \citep{deng2020fewevent} by event type. Together, the suite covers six different criteria, which is what makes it a fair test of a method that is supposed to work for any stated criterion.

\subsection{Benchmark Coverage and Comparability Across Methods}\label{sec:comparability}

An extensive survey of published literature reveals that, although a thorough suite of shared benchmarks exists, coverage is fragmented. Each algorithm discussed in \S\ref{sec:llmloop} is only evaluated on a subset of these benchmarks, and the subsets overlap surprisingly little. Across the 14 method variants and 19 benchmarks considered in this work, only $33\%$ have been reported. Algorithm coverage is also relatively uneven across the benchmark suite. Banking77 and CLINC(I) evaluate 12 of the 14 variants, while benchmarks such as BBC News, DBPedia, M-CID, SNIPS, StackOverflow, and Tweet89 evaluate only two or fewer. However, the evaluation metrics are more consistent than the coverage. Every method reports ACC and NMI, and therefore this work focuses on those two. Figures~\ref{fig:matrix-acc} and~\ref{fig:matrix-nmi} in Appendix~\ref{app:benchmarks} show every published ACC and NMI value by method variant and benchmark.

Another important caveat is that the underlying encoders and LLMs vary significantly across reported algorithms. Results are reported on DistilBERT, INSTRUCTOR, E5, proprietary OpenAI embeddings, or no encoder at all. Subsample sizes, seed counts, and reporting precision also vary across papers. Granularity can also be handled slightly differently. Most methods rely on a known $k$, while others infer granularity from the data itself, which is a significantly harder condition to evaluate against.

A final inconsistency across the literature is benchmark naming and exact which exact documents each method is evaluated against. For example, ClusterLLM re-split the arXiv, Reddit and StackExchange corpora, yielding benchmark granularities (93 arXiv subcategories, 121 StackExchange communities) that differ from the MTEB versions of the same-named benchmarks \citep{zhang2023clusterllm}. The benchmark ``StackOverflow'' may also denote either the 20-class short-title set \citep{xu2017selftaught} or a StackExchange community task across different research. As such, two seemingly matched evaluations may not actually be comparable at all.

This work argues that a fair comparison of methods requires evaluation on the exact same setup (documents, encoders and LLMs). A comparison based only on headline results is therefore not rigorous. To the author's knowledge, no published work in this literature has applied such a matched protocol systematically as of August 2026. This work therefore develops a rigorous testing procedure that accounts for these variables.

\section{LLMs as Evaluators}\label{sec:llmjudge}

\subsection{The LLM-as-a-Judge Paradigm}\label{sec:judgeparadigm}

In addition to their use inside pipelines for algorithms across multiple domains, LLMs have been shown to be useful as evaluators of such pipelines and thus show promise as replacements for expensive human evaluation. \citet{zheng2023judging} showed close correspondence between GPT-4 judgements and human preferences on open-ended generation at rates comparable to human--human agreement. This result established the ``LLM-as-a-judge'' paradigm. LLMs have also been shown to match and even exceed crowd workers on classification in certain tasks \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt}. Interestingly, this growing field has rarely been applied to clustering, although there is significant motivation for its implementation. Human judgements of topic quality have, for example, been shown to diverge from the statistical measures used to evaluate topic models \citep{chang2009tealeaves}. Notably, ProxAnn \citep{hoyle2025proxann} replaces human annotators with LLMs that execute use-oriented judging protocols for clustering output and shows that their results correlate well with those of humans on the same tasks. However, no large-scale, clustering-specific investigation has been performed on this matter. Additionally, at the time of writing, this work has not identified any literature that employs an LLM evaluator inside the clustering loop itself as a signal for choosing between candidate clusterings. This is investigated in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}.

\subsection{Documented Biases and Failure Modes}\label{sec:judgebiases}

Previous research has shown that LLM-as-a-judge methods (outside clustering) have critical biases that must be accounted for. Specifically, judge models have been shown to exhibit self-enhancement bias (preferring their own outputs), position bias (preferring answers by their order of presentation; \citealp{wang2024fair}) and verbosity bias (preferring longer answers) \citep{zheng2023judging}. These biases can be partly mitigated by calibration strategies such as averaging several judgements in which the model reasons before scoring. Rubric-based judges have also been shown to have a higher preference for LLM-generated over human-written text \citep{liu2023geval}; \citet{gu2024judgesurvey} survey these failure modes systematically. Whether these biases extend to clustering remains largely uninvestigated. Another uninvestigated failure mode in clustering is the circularity that may arise when the same LLM is used to both cluster and evaluate a clustering. This breaks independence since the pipeline could possibly raise its own judge score by moving closer to whatever the model already believes.

\section{Research Gap}\label{sec:gap}

This work addresses four deficits in current text clustering research.

First, there is a clear lack of label-free clustering-quality validation metrics that is shown to correlate with human judgement. The only widely used label-free metrics in clustering are geometry-based (\S\ref{sec:labelfree}) and have been demonstrated ineffective in the NLP regime due to the lack of compact, separable clusters (\S\ref{sec:discrepancies}). It remains untested whether LLM-human agreement persists when geometric compactness and separation are controlled. This discrepancy prevents evaluation or tuning of state-of-the-art clustering models in production environments. Research question 1 addresses this challenge.

Second, granularity is typically treated as a fixed input. As such, many state-of-the-art methods require the ground-truth $k$. Classical methods for estimating $k$ therefore also start from this assumption, which contradicts multi-granular text corpora (e.g.\ CLINC utterances form 10 domain clusters or 151 intent clusters). Granularity in a natural language context is therefore a function of the criterion used for clustering. The current literature offers little research on whether an LLM, given a clustering criterion, can recover the annotator-selected granularity. Determining whether $k$ can be inferred establishes whether an LLM-powered clustering pipeline will always require it as a manual input. Research question 2 explores this problem.

Third, no current state-of-the-art LLM-in-the-loop algorithm allows the clustering criterion to be fully exploited. Instruction-tuned encoders \citep{su2023instructor} condition the representation on the criterion but leave the clustering objective and LLM refinement unconditioned. Additionally, an LLM is mostly inserted into the pipeline for pre-clustering representation augmentation and post-clustering refinement. Methods that employ LLMs directly within the clustering process do so by enhancing a single given clustering algorithm such as k-means. No current method leverages the reasoning abilities of LLMs to make active decisions on which enhanced version of different types of clustering algorithms is optimal. Research question 3 addresses this by using the LLM as a criterion-aware partitioner and judge.

The final gap in current research does not concern the clustering methods themselves, but rather the lack of fair comparison between them. Current state-of-the-art results lack direct comparability because benchmark coverage is completely fragmented (see Figures~\ref{fig:matrix-acc} and~\ref{fig:matrix-nmi} in Appendix~\ref{app:benchmarks}). Existing methods with state-of-the-art results also rely on different encoders and LLMs. The values in \S\ref{sec:sota} therefore serve as reference points rather than a standardised ranking. To resolve this, in research question 4, as much effort as possible is put into making a fair matched same-benchmark, same-LLM, and same-encoder comparison to other algorithms in the current literature.

\section{Research Question 1}

\emph{Given only the criterion underlying a human-generated clustering of a corpus, do the scores of an LLM acting as a judge correlate with human judgement? Is any such correlation more than a restatement of what label-free geometric metrics already measure?}  \\

Human judgement of a candidate clustering of a corpus in this methodology is approached as the agreement (calculated using Adjusted Mutual Information) with the human-generated benchmark clustering of that corpus itself. The benchmark's labels record how human annotators (using the criterion) actually clustered the corpus. As such, we can say that a candidate clustering is judged well by human standards if it agrees with those labels. The question then becomes whether the LLM's scores correlate with each candidate's AMI with those human-generated benchmark labels. Importantly, geometric quality is a confound that needs to be taken into account. A candidate with compact, well-separated clusters in embedding space could score high for both the judge and the benchmark labels for the same latent geometric reason, which would inflate this correlation.

These questions are answered in four stages. First, an LLM-as-a-judge algorithm is proposed, which takes a clustering criterion and a candidate clustering of a corpus as input and outputs a score for that clustering. Second, an extensive test bed of candidate clusterings is generated for each of the 19 benchmark corpora. Third, we evaluate these same clusterings using purely geometric metrics. Finally, we measure how strongly the judge's scores correlate with each clustering's agreement against the benchmark's labels and repeat that method with the geometric metrics controlled for, so that any correlation that survives this control cannot be explained by geometry alone. The whole analysis is then repeated with five different judge LLMs to test whether the answer depends on the model.

\subsection{The Codebook Judge}\label{sec:CODEBOOKJUDGE}

\subsubsection{Design}

The codebook judge is designed to take as input both a candidate clustered corpus $D$ where each document is hard-assigned by $y : D \to \{1,\dots,k\}$ to one of $k$ clusters, and a one-sentence clustering criterion $c$. These clustering criteria are unique to the input corpus and in this work, each benchmark has been given a pre-determined criterion sentence. For example, any candidate clustering $y$ of the Banking77 corpus is input into the codebook judge algorithm alongside the criterion sentence: ``which banking customer-service intent the query expresses''. A full breakdown of the exact criterion sentences for each of the 19 benchmarks can be found in Table~\ref{tab:criteria} (Appendix~\ref{app:config}). Each criterion sentence was written by the author based on each corresponding benchmark's own documentation description of the labelled documents. Concretely, the annotation target described in each benchmark's dataset paper was rephrased into a criterion sentence of the form ``which \emph{category type} the \emph{document type} expresses or belongs to''. Thus the sentence names only the organising principle and the kind of document and deliberately says nothing about the number or the names of the categories. All 19 criterion sentences in Table~\ref{tab:criteria} follow the same grammatical template, so that any of them can be inserted verbatim into the fixed prompt templates used throughout the pipeline. The codebook judge is then designed to take these two inputs and output an AMI-based score, typically between 0 and 1, for the candidate clustering.

The judge algorithm itself follows a simple three stage process. First, 300 random (unlabelled) documents $S$ are sampled from the input corpus $D$. These documents are then combined with the clustering criterion $c$ into a single prompt using the template in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-codebook} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. For a given corpus and criterion, this prompt is then fed into a chosen LLM with reasoning enabled, producing the codebook $B$. Once this codebook is generated, 1{,}000 documents $S'$ (disjoint from the 300 sampled before) are again sampled uniformly at random from the corpus, and each document is assigned to one of the codebook categories using the prompt template in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-assign} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}, which has been populated with the codebook categories/definitions of $B$ generated previously and the clustering criterion $c$ itself. As such, one LLM call (reasoning disabled) is made per sampled document, which returns a single category id, with 0 reserved for ``fits none'' documents that the LLM has decided do not fit any of the category members. This stage then results in a reference clustering $R$ of $(1{,}000 - n_{\text{fits-none}})$ documents. Two more hard-coded diagnostics are also applied to this reference clustering in order to maintain its quality. First, no single category is allowed to contain more than 50\% of the documents, and second, no more than 20\% of the documents are allowed to be categorised as ``fits none''. If either of these criteria is not met, the output of the algorithm will include a degeneracy warning to the user.

Finally, to compare this reference cluster assignment $R$ and the candidate clustering $y$, all documents in the candidate that are not included in the reference are discarded, leaving the shared document set $D^{\ast}$. This allows us to make comparisons between both using label-free evaluation metrics. The Adjusted Mutual Information (\S\ref{sec:labelbased}) between the reference and candidate cluster assignments over $D^{\ast}$ is then calculated as the judge's output score since it allows for different numbers of clusters between clusterings. This final step requires no further LLM calls, and as such, for a given corpus, the reference labelling $R$ is calculated once. Using this reference, many different candidate clusterings can be evaluated at no additional LLM cost.

Any LLM can be used for this algorithm. In this methodology the full protocol is run with five different judge LLMs, all at temperature zero: {\small\ttfamily gemini-2.5-flash} \citep{geminiteam2025gemini25}, {\small\ttfamily glm-5} \citep{zhipu2026glm5}, {\small\ttfamily gpt-4o} and {\small\ttfamily gpt-4o-mini} \citep{openai2024gpt4o}, and {\small\ttfamily gpt-3.5-turbo} \citep{openai2023gpt35}. Assignment is one call per document for {\small\ttfamily gemini-2.5-flash} and runs in batches of 20 documents per call, with a batched variant of the assignment prompt, for the other four judges.\footnote{Two model-specific deviations apply. For {\small\ttfamily glm-5}, reasoning is disabled on the codebook call, as its reasoning traces regularly overran the reply limit. For {\small\ttfamily gpt-3.5-turbo}, whose context window is smaller, discovery documents are clipped to 150 characters on long-document corpora, and a codebook reply that repeatedly truncated falls back to accumulating the codebook over 20-document chunks.} This algorithm design is summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:judge}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The codebook judge}
\label{alg:judge}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require corpus $D$; criterion sentence $c$; candidate clustering $y : D \to \{1,\dots,k\}$
\Ensure  judge score for $y$
\Statex
\Procedure{BuildReference}{$D$, $c$}
\State $S \gets$ 300 documents sampled uniformly from $D$
\State $B \gets \Call{LLM}{\text{codebook-discovery prompt}(S, c)}$ \Comment{reasoning on (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-codebook})}
\State $S' \gets$ 1{,}000 documents sampled uniformly from $D \setminus S$
\For{each document $d \in S'$}
\State $r(d) \gets \Call{LLM}{\text{assignment prompt}(d, B, c)}$ \Comment{reasoning off (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-assign})}
\EndFor
\State $R \gets \{(d, r(d)) : d \in S',\; r(d) > 0\}$ \Comment{the reference labelling}
\State \Return $R$
\EndProcedure
\Statex
\Procedure{Judge}{$y$, $R$}
\State $D^\ast \gets$ documents appearing in both $R$ and $y$
\State \Return $\mathrm{AMI}\big(R|_{D^\ast},\, y|_{D^\ast}\big)$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Corpora}

In order to maintain consistency in this work's methodology, the same exact 19 corpora discussed in \S\ref{sec:suite} are used to test all four research questions in order to increase methodological consistency while also ensuring diversity across intent, domain, topic, entity, relation, and clustering criteria. The human-annotation labels included in each benchmark corpus in this section are only used in order to compare to human-reference metrics and are never ``seen'' by the codebook judge algorithm. All 19 corpora are embedded once with a single frozen encoder ({\small\ttfamily instructor-large}) with the generic instruction \emph{``Represent the sentence(s) for clustering: ''}, and these same encodings are shared by every candidate clustering and every geometric metric below in order to maintain methodological consistency. All embedding vectors in this work are additionally $\ell_2$-normalised to unit length, and whenever a distance in representation space is mentioned, it refers to the cosine distance between these normalised vectors. On unit-length vectors, Euclidean distance is a monotone function of cosine distance, so the Euclidean objectives of the clustering algorithms optimise the same orderings as this cosine distance.

\subsection{Candidate Clusterings}

In order to evaluate the correlation between the judge's scores and each candidate's agreement with the benchmark's human clustering, a diverse test set of different clusterings of all 19 benchmarks was produced. These allow for a base of candidate clusterings of different qualities, granularities and corpora for which the benchmark labelling is known and which we can then evaluate using the judge algorithm and the benchmark labels. For each candidate, we generated a set of five algorithms, one from each family of geometric bias reviewed in \S\ref{sec:algorithms} (k-means, a Gaussian mixture model, spectral clustering, Ward's agglomerative clustering and Leiden community detection), crossed with five granularities ($k_{\mathrm{bench}} \times \tfrac14, \tfrac12, 1, 2, 4$) and three random seeds, all on the identical frozen embeddings. This yielded 1{,}222 candidate clusterings across the 19 corpora (after removing the runs that received a warning sign from the codebook judge algorithm).

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}\label{sec:RQ1_STATPROT}

The primary metric used to quantify the correlation between the judge's scores and human evaluation is the Spearman rank correlation coefficient, $\rho$, between the judge's scores and each candidate's AMI with respect to the benchmark labels. This value of $\rho$ is calculated by first converting both quantities for all $n$ candidates to ranks (from low to high) such that, for a given benchmark, each candidate clustering $i$ is assigned an AMI with respect to the benchmark and a judge score (its AMI with respect to the LLM's reference clustering). The coefficient is then calculated as the Pearson correlation between these two rank sequences. In the absence of tied ranks, this is equivalent to
\[
\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta_i^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)},
\]
where $\delta_i$ is the difference between candidate $i$'s two ranks. A $\rho$ of $+1$ means the judge orders the candidates by AMI exactly as their benchmark agreement does. A $\rho$ of $0$ means there is no monotonic relationship, and a negative $\rho$ means the judge tends to prefer worse candidates, so an inverse relationship exists. In addition to this coefficient, which is calculated for each benchmark, the permutation $p$-value is also calculated by randomly shuffling the judge scores 10{,}000 times and recalculating $\rho$ each time. The $p$-value is then the fraction of shuffles that produce a correlation at least as extreme as the observed one, representing the probability, under the null hypothesis of no association, of obtaining such a correlation by chance. If, for a given benchmark, the $p$-value associated with its $\rho$ value is smaller than the chosen significance level, $\alpha = 0.05$, the correlation is considered statistically significant.

For the geometry-controlled analysis, first the silhouette score and the Cali\'nski--Harabasz index are calculated for each candidate clustering of a given benchmark. For a given geometry control, say the silhouette score, the analysis has three steps. First, the judge scores, AMI versus benchmark scores, and the geometry control are converted to ranks, like before. Second, a linear regression model is fitted by least squares to predict each candidate's judge rank from its control rank. For each candidate, the residual on the final model is kept:
\[
e_i^{\mathrm{judge}} \;=\; \mathrm{rk}(J_i) - \bigl(a\,\mathrm{rk}(C_i) +b\bigr),
\]
where $\mathrm{rk}(J_i)$ and $\mathrm{rk}(C_i)$ are candidate $i$'s judge and control ranks, and $a, b$ are the fitted line's coefficients. This process is repeated for AMI versus benchmark, and the residual $e_i^{\mathrm{AMI}}$ is computed in the same way. Now that we have two residuals per candidate, we repeat the same process from above, where a Spearman rank correlation coefficient and an associated $p$-value are calculated between both residuals. If the judge's scores were simply a restatement of the geometric metric, the fitted line would predict them almost perfectly; the residuals would be close to random noise, and therefore their correlation would be close to zero. Any correlation that remains actually shows the agreement between the judge and human ranking that the controlled geometric metric is unable to account for. The same method is additionally applied with both geometric metrics controlled at once. In this joint version, the regression predicts each rank from the ranks of the silhouette score and the Cali\'nski--Harabasz index together. A correlation that survives this joint control therefore cannot be explained by either geometric metric alone or by both together.

Importantly, these analyses are done on a per-benchmark basis, and at no point are all the candidates pooled. This was a deliberate choice in order to mitigate the risk of confounding due to Simpson's paradox. Simpson's paradox is a statistical phenomenon where a clear trend observed within isolated groups disappears or even reverses when the data is pooled. In this context, the choice of benchmark itself could act as a confounding variable. These benchmarks have different attributes, such as clustering difficulty and the number of documents. If all data were pooled, some candidates evaluated on easy benchmarks could, for instance, falsely appear to outperform a better candidate who was simply evaluated on a more rigorous benchmark, and this could then completely change a trend. By restricting to a per-benchmark basis, we ensure that these confounding variables do not influence the outcome. The single headline result was therefore not chosen to be the $\rho$ over all candidates pooled, but instead the median over all benchmark-specific correlation coefficients.

Finally, because an LLM call is inherently random, every judge run was repeated ten times independently, with each repetition drawing a fresh discovery sample, codebook, and reference labelling. Each benchmark-specific coefficient entering the median above is the mean of that benchmark's ten per-run coefficients. The run-to-run standard deviation of each reported statistic, recomputed within each repetition, is reported alongside it, and a benchmark's correlation is considered significant when it reaches $p < 0.05$ in a majority of the ten repetitions.

\section{Research Question 2}

\emph{To what extent can LLMs, given a clustering criterion, infer the granularity of purpose-built human taxonomies from unlabelled data?}\\

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:estimatek}, the granularity of a corpus clustering is ambiguous in itself and depends on the criterion by which the clustering is performed. This experiment asks whether the natural language reasoning of LLMs, given that criterion, can be exploited to resolve the ambiguity. Eight LLM-based granularity estimators are compared: two are adaptations of published methods, one adapts a classical diversity estimator to LLM judgements, and five are of this work's own design. Each method runs on the full benchmark suite with multiple independent repetitions, and the inferred granularities $\hat{k}$ are compared to the benchmark granularities $k$ to test whether the criterion alone is enough for an LLM to infer the granularity a human chose.

\subsection{Experimental Design}\label{sec:RQ2_DESIGN}

Eight estimation methods are run on each of the 19 corpora in the benchmark suite, with ten independent repetitions per pair for a total of $1{,}520$ runs. For all methods, the same frozen encoder ({\small\ttfamily instructor-large} with the generic instruction \emph{``Represent the sentence(s) for clustering: ''}) and LLM ({\small\ttfamily gemini-2.5-flash} at temperature zero) are used. Additionally, the exact same benchmark-specific criterion sentences (Table~\ref{tab:criteria} in Appendix~\ref{app:config}) are applied as before. Like before, LLM calls that generate a single quality-critical output (a codebook, a final deduplication, a numeric estimate) run with the model's reasoning enabled and simple yes/no comparison calls run with reasoning disabled. To test whether the findings depend on the LLM, the full eight-method protocol is additionally rerun with {\small\ttfamily gpt-4o-mini} and {\small\ttfamily gpt-3.5-turbo}.

\subsubsection{Granularity Inference Methods}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Codebook:} The Codebook method replicates the first stage of the codebook judge of research question 1 exactly, where an LLM is prompted to generate a codebook of categories based on a 300-document sample and the criterion (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-codebook} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The inferred $\hat{k}$ is then simply taken as the number of categories.
\item \textbf{Direct:} The Direct method is a simplification of the Codebook method in which an LLM is shown 300 documents and the criterion but is simply prompted to guess the number of categories directly. The full prompt is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-direct} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.
\item \textbf{Probe:} The Probe method is an extension of the Codebook method where the LLM is shown the same 300 documents and criterion, but is asked to generate three different codebooks: one with ``coarse granularity'', one with ``fine granularity'' and one with ``natural granularity'' (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-probe} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The output number of clusters $\hat{k}$ is then chosen to be the number of categories of the one with natural granularity. This forces the LLM to reason about different possible notions of granularity for the same criterion instead of generating a codebook in one shot.
\item \textbf{Pairwise:} The Pairwise estimator is an adaptation from the ClusterLLM \citep{zhang2023clusterllm} algorithm. First, a grid of candidate granularities is constructed as 25 logarithmically spaced points on $[2, 200]$ rounded to integers for a total of 23 unique $\hat{k}$ values:
\[
\mathcal{K} = \{2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 14, 17, 20, 24, 29, 36, 43, 52, 63, 77, 93, 112, 136, 165, 200\}.
\]
A Ward hierarchy is then built over a 2{,}000-document subsample of the corpus embeddings. At each level $k \in \mathcal{K}$, the two clusters that merge at that level are identified. From each cluster pair, three document pairs are then sampled that straddle the two clusters. Each document pair is then fed into a prompt template that is used to ask an LLM whether it belongs to the same category as per the clustering criterion as a yes/no question. This exact ``yes/no'' prompt can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-same} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. The same process is then repeated at the two adjacent, finer merges, from $k+2$ to $k+1$ clusters and from $k+3$ to $k+2$ clusters, so that every grid level $k \in \mathcal{K}$ aggregates nine yes/no votes drawn from three neighbouring merges of the hierarchy. From the pooled nine votes at each grid level, the share of ``no'' answers is calculated. This fraction represents the proportion of merges at that granularity the LLM considers ``unjustified''. Importantly, this share is expected to be high at low $k$, where merges join unrelated categories, and low at high $k$, where merges join fragments of the same category. Finally, a logistic curve of this fraction against $\log k$ is fitted and the final $\hat{k}$ is chosen to be the point where it crosses 50\% (which is then rounded and clipped to the nearest $k$ in the grid range). This $\hat{k}$ can therefore be thought of as the point between where the LLM believes the clustering is too granular and where it thinks it is not granular enough, which is by construction the point of optimal granularity.
\item \textbf{Sequential:} The Sequential estimator is adapted from the TopicGPT algorithm \citep{pham2024topicgpt}. In this method, 600 random samples from the corpus are streamed into the LLM in batches of 20. At each new batch, the LLM (reasoning disabled) gets as input the new batch, the criterion and the running category list and may add only genuinely new categories required by documents that fit none of the existing ones. After three consecutive batches add nothing to the running list of categories, the process is stopped. Finally, the LLM (reasoning enabled) is prompted to merge duplicate and near-duplicate categories to make sure that the same category found under different possible names in different batches is never counted more than once, which would artificially inflate the number of categories. The final $\hat{k}$ is then chosen as the length of the final list. Both the batchwise prompt and the deduplication prompt can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-sequential} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.
\item \textbf{Simpson:} The Simpson estimator is an adaptation of a metric from the field of ecology, originally introduced to quantify the concentration of a population across species \citep{simpson1949measurement}. For a label distribution $p_1, \ldots, p_k$, the Simpson index $\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i^2$ is the probability that two randomly drawn documents share a category (where originally it was the probability that two randomly drawn individuals belong to the same species), and for $k$ equally sized categories $p_i = 1/k$ gives $\lambda = 1/k$. In order to calculate $\hat{k}$ this way, first 120 random sample document pairs are taken from the corpus. For each pair, an LLM is then prompted (see Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-same} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}) to vote on whether they belong to the same or a different cluster, given the criterion. The resulting estimate $\widehat{P}(\text{same})$ is taken as the fraction of ``same'' answers, and this is then used to yield $\hat{k} = 1/\widehat{P}(\text{same})$. One main bias in this method is that in our case, the same-cluster size assumption is clearly wrong. Therefore, the estimator is systematically biased downward, and we include it as a relatively low-cost baseline.
\item \textbf{Judge sweep:} The Judge sweep method is a hybrid between classical clustering and an LLM-based method, in which we run a simple k-means clustering algorithm at every value in $\mathcal{K}$, and each partition is scored by the judge of research question 1. The $\hat{k}$ is simply chosen as the one that corresponds to the highest judge score.
\item \textbf{Merge convergence:} This is again a hybrid method that combines k-means with LLM reasoning. First, we run an over-segmented k-means model with $k_0 = 200$. We then iteratively merge these micro-clusters until we reach an optimal $k$. We do this by first summarising each micro-cluster by its three most central documents in embedding space. Then, the following is repeated for multiple rounds. First, we calculate the distance between all centroids of the micro-clusters. Second, we identify the eight closest cluster pairs by centroid distance. Then, we prompt an LLM with the three-document summary of each micro-cluster to determine whether to merge them. If the LLM reasons they should be merged, we merge them. This repeated algorithm stops once a single round results in no more merges. The final amount of clusters is then taken as $\hat{k}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Design Rationale}

Because the literature offers no established methodologies for leveraging LLMs for $k$-inference, this work tested a variety of methods in an attempt to cover a diverse set of possible approaches. The eight estimator methods were chosen to cover a wide range of distinct possible routes by which an LLM could be used to infer $k$: enumerating categories and counting them (Codebook, Sequential); naming a number outright (Direct, Probe); aggregating many local same/different judgements into a total estimate (Pairwise, Simpson); and combining the model's judgement with the embedding geometry so that the estimate corresponds to an actual clustering (Judge sweep, Merge convergence). In this sense, many bases are covered, but no fully exhaustive search of all possible LLM methods is performed.

\subsection{Statistical Protocol} \label{sec:RQ2_STATPROT}

The inferred values $\hat{k}$ are compared to the benchmark values $k$ using summary statistics, a calibration analysis, and a paired test against an LLM-free baseline, as follows. To reiterate: the actual benchmark $k$ values are never seen by the $k$-inference methods and are used only to statistically evaluate the results.

Per corpus, each method runs at ten different random seeds, and each repetition reruns every stochastic element of the method.  These stochastic elements include the document samples shown to the model, the subsample for the Ward hierarchy, any random document pairs, and the k-means initialisations. For the Codebook and Judge sweep methods, the ten repetitions are the ten independent judge runs of the research question 1 judge (independent random discovery sample, codebook, and reference labelling). All per-corpus results are the median estimate over the ten repetitions, and we keep the individual estimates for the stability analysis.

Six main summary statistics are reported to answer this research question. The first statistic is the median signed error between $\hat{k}$ and $k$. The benchmark granularities over all 19 chosen benchmarks span more than one order of magnitude. As such, the individual error between a single inferred $\hat{k}$ and benchmark $k$ was chosen as the log-ratio error:
\[
\varepsilon = \log_2\!\left(\hat{k} / k\right),
\]
where the sign of the error represents whether the inference overshot the benchmark ($\varepsilon > 0$) or undershot it ($\varepsilon < 0$). Here, the magnitude of $\varepsilon$ counts factor-of-two steps. A correctly inferred $\hat{k} = k$ will correspond to $\varepsilon = 0$, one that is double or half the benchmark $k$ has $|\varepsilon| = 1$, and one that is four times too large or too small has $|\varepsilon| = 2$ and so on. For each corpus, we compute one error from the median estimate over the ten repetitions, giving 19 errors per method. The median signed error for a method is the median of these 19 per-corpus errors. Second, the median absolute error for a given method is the median of the absolute values $|\varepsilon|$ of these 19 per-corpus errors. Third, the fraction of corpora on which the median estimate for a method lands within a factor of $1.5$ of the benchmark $k$ is calculated as the fraction of the 19 per-corpus errors for which $|\varepsilon| \le \log_2 1.5$. The fourth statistic is a stricter version of this count, the fraction of corpora on which the median estimate lands within ten percent of the benchmark $k$, that is $|\hat{k} - k| \le 0.1\,k$.

The fifth metric is Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) between $\log_2 \hat{k}$ and $\log_2 k$ across the 19 corpora, where $\hat{k}$ is each corpus's median estimate over the ten repetitions \citep{lin1989concordance}. The CCC measures how well the pairs of inferred and benchmark granularities agree. In other words, it measures how closely the 19 points $(\log_2 k, \log_2 \hat{k})$ fall on the identity line $\hat{k} = k$. A CCC of $1$ means all the inferred $\hat{k}$ are in perfect agreement with benchmark $k$, while a CCC near $0$ means no agreement at all. Between these extremes, the CCC goes down for random scatter and systematic deviation from the identity line $\hat{k} = k$. More exactly, the CCC metric is calculated as follows:
\[
\mathrm{CCC} \;=\; \frac{2\,s_{xy}}{s_x^2 + s_y^2 + \left(\bar{x} - \bar{y}\right)^2},
\]
where, for each of the 19 corpora $i$, $x_i = \log_2 k_i$ is its benchmark granularity and $y_i = \log_2 \hat{k}_i$ its inferred granularity (the median estimate over the ten repetitions). The means $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$, the variances $s_x^2$ and $s_y^2$, and the covariance $s_{xy}$ are all calculated across these 19 corpus-level pairs.

The final metric is calculated as the spread over ten seeds. This spread is the range of $\hat{k}$ across the ten repetitions divided by its median, per corpus. A spread near one thus means the estimate typically swings by its own size under resampling.

In order to analyse the calibration of the inferred granularities against the benchmark values, we regressed $\log_2 \hat{k}$ on $\log_2 k$ across the 19 corpora for each method. The slope of this fit measures the degree to which changes of $k$ across corpora are tracked by the corresponding inferred $\hat{k}$. A perfectly calibrated estimator would simply have a slope of 1, and an estimator that returns the same number regardless of the corpus would have slope 0. Uncertainty on the fitted line was calculated using a bootstrap over the corpora (2{,}000 resamples of the 19 benchmarks with replacement), from which a 95\% confidence band is built. In each bootstrap resample, the regression is refitted on the redrawn set of these corpora, and the confidence band shows the central 95\% of the resulting fitted lines at every point.

Additionally, we compare all methods against a non-LLM baseline $k$-inference method. The baseline is chosen to be the silhouette-argmax method (\S\ref{sec:estimatek}), in which we test the same series of candidate $k$ values $\mathcal{K}$ as before and take the one with the highest silhouette score (\S\ref{sec:labelfree}) on k-means for that corpus as the inferred $k$. The baseline is run under the same protocol as the other methods.

\section{Research Question 3}

\emph{To what extent can an LLM, acting as both partitioner and judge, equipped with prior knowledge of the clustering criterion, and inserted into the clustering pipeline, align unsupervised clustering with the partitions humans would draw?}\\

In order to answer this research question, this work introduces CLEAR (Clustering with LLM Evaluation And Repair), a novel LLM text clustering algorithm purpose-built around this research question. CLEAR is a criterion-conditioned clustering pipeline that introduces an LLM at several stages. On the one hand, in line with research question 1, an LLM serves as an active judge to select from a list of candidate base (classical) clustering algorithms (e.g.\ k-means, GMM, etc.). On the other hand, the LLM acts as a partitioner to enhance the chosen base clustering algorithm through techniques seen before, such as pre-clustering document rewrites, generating category examples and post-clustering boundary repair. CLEAR is an oracle-$k$ algorithm, meaning it requires an input for granularity. This aligns with other state-of-the-art algorithms in the literature. This algorithm was run on all 19 benchmarks over multiple seed rounds, and a full statistical analysis was performed.

\subsection{The CLEAR Algorithm}

CLEAR takes three inputs. The first input is the unlabelled corpus $D$. The second is the criterion sentence $c$ which again is the exact same one-sentence statement used throughout this thesis (Table~\ref{tab:criteria} in Appendix~\ref{app:config}). The third is the input granularity $k$. This granularity $k$ was chosen to be user-controlled rather than inferred following the main conclusion of research question 2. The algorithm then simply outputs a partition of the corpus $D$ into $k$ hard-assigned clusters.

The clustering pipeline, visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:clear-pipeline}, has three main stages in which LLM calls are made. First, a suite of candidate base clusterings is generated. These include relatively simple cluster assignments, including classical non-LLM methods, as well as slightly LLM-enhanced versions of base algorithms. Second, an LLM judge similar to the codebook judge discussed in \S\ref{sec:CODEBOOKJUDGE} scores each method in the suite on how well it clusters the input corpus $D$. The base algorithm with the highest judge score is then chosen. The final stage then uses the LLM to refine the partitions as a post-clustering (boundary repair) step. CLEAR is also designed such that the chosen base encoder and LLM are user-controlled inputs.

\subsubsection{Candidate Generation}

The candidate suite consists of eight different candidate base-clustering algorithms at the user-specified $k$. The suite was chosen to have maximal diversity across techniques in three different directions: algorithm family, embedding space, and initialisation. The eight candidates are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{k-means (raw embeddings)}: k-means applied directly to the raw corpus embeddings $X_r$ produced by the chosen input encoder.
\item \textbf{k-means (rewritten embeddings)}: For each document in the corpus, an LLM is first prompted to rewrite it in a phrase of at most 15 words that captures only the criterion instead of lexical noise such as tone, detail or phrasing. These rewritten documents are then encoded as $X_w$, and k-means is run on these embeddings. The exact prompt can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-rewrite} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.
\item \textbf{k-means (averaged embeddings)}: This method combines the raw and rewritten embeddings, where each document's raw and rewritten embedding vectors are averaged element-wise and $\ell_2$-normalised to produce the averaged embeddings $X_c$, on which k-means is then run.
\item \textbf{exemplar-initialised (raw embeddings)}: Simple k-means on $X_r$, but the initialisation centroids are generated by an LLM instead of being randomly chosen. The $k$ initial centroids are generated in three steps. First, using the exact same prompt as the codebook judge from research question 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-codebook} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}), an LLM is used to generate a codebook at the LLM's chosen granularity. Second, for every category in this codebook, the LLM is prompted to provide five short example documents that would clearly belong to it (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-exemplar} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). These five short examples are encoded, and their embeddings are averaged and $\ell_2$-normalised to generate the initialisations. The LLM codebook generally includes either more or fewer categories than the user-specified $k$. When the codebook $k$ is higher than the user-specified $k$, a separate k-means algorithm is run on the initialisations to reduce the number of initialisations to the user-specified $k$. If the codebook $k$ is lower than the user-specified $k$, all initial centroids are kept. Each missing centroid is then generated one by one by sampling from the documents with a probability proportional to their squared distance from the nearest centroid chosen so far. This filling procedure therefore produces initialisation centroids in the regions the codebook does not cover. This filling method is very similar to the k-means++ algorithm discussed in \S\ref{sec:algorithms}.
\item\textbf{exemplar-initialised (averaged embeddings)}: This method repeats the exact same methodology as exemplar-initialised (raw), but instead starting from the averaged embeddings $X_c$.
\item \textbf{GMM (raw embeddings)}: A Gaussian Mixture Model with diagonal covariance is run using the user-specified $k$ on 50-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) projections of the $X_r$ embeddings, and each document is then hard-assigned to the component with the highest posterior probability.
\item \textbf{GMM (averaged embeddings)}: The same exact procedure as GMM (raw), but instead using the $X_c$ embeddings.
\item \textbf{Ward (raw embeddings)}: Agglomerative clustering with Ward's linkage cut at $k$ on the 50-dimensional PCA projection of $X_r$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Judge}

For a given corpus, we now have eight ways to divide the corpus into $k$ clusters. At this point, we score each candidate clustering using the same codebook judge described in \S\ref{sec:CODEBOOKJUDGE} and rank them from lowest to highest score with one small refinement. Judge scores inherently carry some amount of random noise. As such, two candidates with judge scores within $0.01$ are treated as ties. We resolve these ties by picking the candidate with the least LLM involvement as the winner. The reason we do this is to mitigate any bias the LLM judge may hold towards LLM-generated answers. From least to most LLM involvement, ties are resolved in the order of k-means (raw), GMM (raw), exemplar-initialised (raw), GMM (averaged), exemplar-initialised (averaged), k-means (averaged), k-means (rewritten), and Ward (raw).

\subsubsection{Boundary Repair}

The chosen candidate clustering of the embedded documents is then refined using an LLM. First, each cluster is named by prompting an LLM once per cluster to choose an appropriate cluster title based on the criterion and the eight documents with embeddings closest to the centroid in cosine distance. The exact prompt can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-name} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We generate these cluster names once and cache them. The cluster refinement then runs for a total of three rounds. In each round, we first select the least confident documents (those points with the smallest difference in distance between their own cluster centroid and the next closest one), excluding documents already selected in previous rounds. These points represent the documents where geometry is most uncertain about their cluster assignment. Thus, these points represent the documents where geometry is most likely to disagree with the partition a human following the criterion would draw, and they are therefore the ones targeted for reassignment by the LLM. The LLM reclassifies each selected document in batches of 20 using the cached category names. If the LLM does not find a cluster for a document, nothing changes. The exact prompt for this step can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-classify} in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. At the end of each round, the judge rescores the full clustering. If the judge score improved, the round's changes are kept, and the next round starts from these changes. If it did not, the changes are discarded, and the next round starts from the previous clustering. As such, for each run of the CLEAR algorithm, an LLM individually assigns the 600 most ``problematic'' documents to their cluster.

\subsection{Baselines and Analyses}

For all 19 corpora, we run the CLEAR algorithm with five different random seeds that affect the 1{,}000-document sample behind the judge's reference labelling and the initialisations of all eight candidates. We build the codebook and LLM-generated texts once per corpus, and keep the model temperature at zero throughout. The actual LLM and embedder used are {\small\ttfamily glm-5} and {\small\ttfamily gemini-embedding-001} \citep{lee2025gemini} respectively. For each run on each corpus, we report NMI and ACC against the benchmark clustering. To further investigate how CLEAR aligned unsupervised clustering with the partitions humans would draw, we conducted a few more specialised analyses.

First, we compare against a geometry-only baseline. Plain k-means is run at the same user-specified $k$ on the same raw document embeddings that CLEAR receives using the same {\small\ttfamily gemini-embedding-001} encoder, and its NMI and ACC against the benchmark clustering are compared to the CLEAR results over the same five random seeds. As such, the geometry-only baseline at each seed is in fact identical to CLEAR's own k-means (raw) candidate at that seed. The per-benchmark mean deltas between the two are tested with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, mirroring the statistical protocol of research question 2.

Second, we analyse the judge's performance within the clustering pipeline compared with other baseline methods. To assess how well the judge performed, for every run of CLEAR (19 benchmarks over five random seed runs, for a total of 95 runs), we score all eight candidate clusterings using NMI and ACC against their corresponding benchmark. Now that we know each candidate's true NMI and ACC, we can compare different candidate-picking methods. The methods include random picking, picking the candidate that maximises silhouette score, the judge's actual pick, and picking by maximum ACC and NMI against the benchmark (an upper bound no selector could exceed). We then summarise each candidate selection strategy by the mean NMI and ACC against the benchmark candidate it selects, averaged over all 95 runs. This lets us compare the judge to other baseline selection methods and, most importantly, to the oracle, showing how close it comes to picking the absolute best candidate. We also record the exact candidates that got chosen and further analyse these data to investigate which parts of the suite the judge chooses most and how its selections vary across corpora.

In the final analysis, we investigate the improvement the post-clustering refinement step makes on the overall algorithm. For every run over all corpora, we evaluate the judge-picked clustering against the benchmark and compare these scores with the full CLEAR algorithm's scores to assess what the final repair rounds contributed.

\section{Research Question 4}

\emph{To what extent can an LLM, acting as both partitioner and judge, equipped with prior knowledge of the clustering criterion, and inserted into the clustering pipeline, align unsupervised clustering with the partitions humans would draw?}\\

In this research question, CLEAR's performance is compared to the SOTA results mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:sota} to evaluate where this work's methods lie in the current literature landscape. To ensure a fair comparison, the evaluation set contains LLM-in-the-loop clustering algorithms only. Methods that rely on training or fine-tuning the encoder itself, such as those tracked by the MTEB benchmark (\S\ref{sec:mteb}), are out of the scope of this thesis. Because no part of the CLEAR algorithm implements fine-tuning, these methods are omitted from the analysis.

\subsection{Matched and Unmatched Configurations}\label{sec:matchedconfigs}

We compare CLEAR directly to SOTA results in two settings: matched and unmatched. In the unmatched configuration, we run CLEAR using the same frozen encoder and LLM as in research question 3 ({\small\ttfamily gemini-embedding-001} and {\small\ttfamily glm-5}) and compare its results on all 19 corpora at face value against published results. This comparison is confounded, as a win against a SOTA method on a benchmark might come from the algorithm or simply from a stronger encoder or LLM than the published method had. To address this, we also run a matched study in which we match the encoder and LLM of the SOTA algorithm we compare against to the CLEAR algorithm (see Table~\ref{tab:rq4-sota-stacks} in Appendix~\ref{app:config}). Where the NMI and ACC records for a benchmark are held by different methods, the NMI holder's configuration is matched. Importantly, the algorithm itself is frozen across both settings. Six matched components are substitutes where the original model can no longer be run or has been superseded, as marked in Table~\ref{tab:rq4-sota-stacks}; for example, the SPILL algorithm's encoder is no longer publicly served and is proxied by {\small\ttfamily instructor-large}. Additionally, we run a simple ablation study in which CLEAR is matched by the LLM but not by the encoder, and conversely, where the encoder is matched and the LLM is left unmatched.

\subsection{Overall Validity Across Judge LLMs}

All five judges correlate strongly or very strongly with the benchmark ranking, and all remain strongly correlated when the silhouette score and the CH index are controlled for. The five medians in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-judgebeds} lie between $+0.86$ ({\small\ttfamily gpt-3.5-turbo}) and $+0.93$ ({\small\ttfamily glm-5}), and the run-to-run standard deviations are between $\pm 0.01$ and $\pm 0.04$. Three of the five judges are significant on all 19 benchmarks ({\small\ttfamily gemini-2.5-flash}, {\small\ttfamily gpt-4o}, and {\small\ttfamily glm-5}), while the other two are significant on 18 of the 19.

The joint geometry control answers the second half of the research question. After controlling for all that the silhouette score and the CH index can explain together (\S\ref{sec:RQ1_STATPROT}), the five medians drop only by $0.06$ to $0.10$, to between $+0.76$ and $+0.87$, so every judge's median remains above the $+0.68$ strong cutoff.

This is clearly visible as the uniform gap between each bar pair.

\subsection{Per-Benchmark Patterns}

Looking at the orange judge-AMI correlation (unnormalised) data in Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-hbars-judges-appendix} in Appendix~\ref{app:results}, it is apparent that the general ordering of correlations of benchmarks is largely the same across the LLMs used. The highest rankings are consistently shared by the topic corpora and the coarser-grained corpora. All six topic corpora (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} in Appendix~\ref{app:litstat}) score $+0.62$ or higher from every judge, and all three domain corpora (MTOP(D), CLINC(D), and MASSIVE(D)) score $+0.86$ or higher. The middle rankings are occupied by the corpora labelled by entity type, relation, and event type, which score between $+0.60$ and $+0.95$. The intent corpora sit at both extremes. SNIPS, M-CID, and MTOP(I) score $+0.94$ or higher from every judge and sit at or near the top of every judge's ranking, while MASSIVE(I), Banking77, and CLINC(I) consistently rank at the bottom, with CLINC(I) between $+0.16$ and $+0.30$. CLINC(I) is also the only benchmark for which any judge fails to show statistical significance.

Closer inspection of the domain/intent pair benchmarks (benchmarks that have the same corpus clustered by both domain and intent at different granularities) shows another disparity. MASSIVE appears twice with the same 2{,}974 documents, yet every judge scores its domain labelling at $+0.86$ to $+0.93$ against only $+0.59$ to $+0.74$ for its intent labelling. CLINC shows an even greater disparity ($+0.89$ to $+0.94$ against $+0.16$ to $+0.30$).

The light blue panels show that controlling for both geometric metrics together does not break the correlation on any benchmark for any judge. Almost all controlled correlations remain in the strong or very strong region, with between one and six benchmarks per judge falling to the moderate region and only a single value below it.

The red gap panels plot the difference between each benchmark's raw and geometry-controlled correlation. A positive gap means geometry explains part of the judge's agreement on that benchmark, so removing it lowers the correlation. In this case, the geometry artificially inflated the correlation of the judge with benchmark rankings. On the other hand, a negative gap means the control raises the correlation and the judge and the benchmark ranking agree better once the geometric part of both is removed. As such, for negative gaps the geometry can be thought of as obscuring the correlation of the judge with benchmark rankings. The distribution of these gaps over the benchmarks is largely the same for all five LLMs with most gaps within about $\pm 0.15$ and the extremes on the same corpora in every row. The largest positive gaps are for Tweet89, between $+0.18$ and $+0.36$ for all five judges, and MTOP(D), between $+0.22$ and $+0.43$ for four of the five with {\small\ttfamily glm-5} the exception at $+0.07$. The largest negative gaps are shown on the weakest intent corpora. For example, CLINC(I) lies between $-0.39$ and $-0.44$ for every judge and Banking77 between $-0.20$ and $-0.29$, so on these two corpora the geometric metrics do not explain the judge's agreement, and the correlation is higher after controlling for the silhouette score and the Cali\'nski--Harabasz index.

\subsection{Estimation Accuracy}

Direct is the most accurate estimator. Its estimates lie within $1.5\times$ of the benchmark $k$ on 9 of 19 corpora with a median absolute error of $0.61$, which corresponds to a typical miss of about a factor of $1.5$ ($2^{0.61} \approx 1.5$). The Pairwise-inferred granularities have the highest concordance (CCC $0.62$), and six of the eight methods reach CCC between $0.42$ and $0.62$. Probe is the clear outlier with a median absolute error $1.61$ and CCC of $0.05$. Most importantly, no estimator (including the non-LLM baseline) places more than 4 of 19 inferred corpora $k$ within $\pm 10\%$ of the benchmark $k$.

None of the eight LLM-based methods outperform the baseline method in terms of the number of corpora within $1.5\times$ or $\pm 10\%$ of the benchmark. The baseline silhouette-argmax method matches the best LLM method (Direct) on the
within-$1.5\times$ rate of 9/19, but with a median absolute error of 1.15, almost double that of Direct (0.61). Its CCC of 0.19 shows that its estimates barely co-vary with the benchmark granularity. As such, its hit rate comes from landing in the middle of the granularity range rather than from tracking $k$. Conversely, the LLM methods' estimates move with the benchmark $k$ even where their absolute accuracy is similar. One striking exception to this is the Probe method which, similarly to the baseline, has a hit rate similar to other LLM methods (within-$1.5\times$ = 6/19) by simply predicting a relatively constant $k$.

The Pairwise, Sequential, and Merge convergence methods systematically overshoot the inferred granularities, while the Codebook, Direct, Probe, Judge sweep and Simpson methods all undershoot, Probe most strongly. Six methods have spreads near $1$ ($1.03$ to $1.14$) so repeated runs disagree by about as much as the estimate itself. Pairwise is significantly steadier (spread = $0.57$) and Merge convergence is by far the most stable (spread = $0.17$) while also being the least accurate (median absolute error $1.73$ and within-$\pm 10\%$ rate of 0/19).

On corpora with a low $k$, the inferred $\hat{k}$ is usually too high, and on corpora with a high $k$, the inferred $\hat{k}$ is usually too low. The methods differ mainly in how strongly the estimates are pulled towards this middle and in how much the estimates scatter around the fitted line (as is apparent from the different widths of the shaded confidence bands).

\subsection{Clustering Quality}\label{sec:results-rq3-quality}

Over the 19 benchmarks, CLEAR reaches a mean NMI of $79.0$ and a mean ACC of $68.6$. Ten benchmarks lie at $80$ NMI or higher, topped by SNIPS ($96.0$ NMI, $98.2$ ACC), MTOP(D) and BBC News, and the lowest NMI values belong to the three mention corpora FewEvent, FewRel and FewNerd ($64$ to $66$) and to CLINC(D) ($59.1$). The runs are relatively stable over the five seeds with most standard deviations below $3$ points. NMI values are generally higher than ACC. Since ACC matches clusters to benchmark classes one-to-one, a clustering that splits a benchmark class loses ACC even when NMI shows the split preserves most of the label information.

\subsection{Improvement over the Geometric Baseline}

CLEAR improves on k-means on 15 of 19 benchmarks in NMI (mean $+2.6$) and 13 of 19 in ACC (mean $+3.7$). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 19 per-benchmark mean deltas confirms the NMI uplift is statistically significant ($W = 34$, $p = 0.012$), while the ACC uplift falls just short of significance ($W = 47$, $p = 0.055$). The largest gains (FewNerd at $+15.8$ NMI and $+14.9$ ACC and StackEx-S2S at $+10.7$ NMI and $+19.4$ ACC) are consistent across seeds and metrics, both with whiskers of about two points or less. The losses are small on NMI, at worst $-3.5$ on CLINC(I), but CLINC(I) also has the one large ACC loss at $-14.1$. The weakest benchmark of the uplift comparison is the finest intent corpus.

\subsection{Selected Candidates}\label{sec:selected_candidates}

Table~\ref{tab:rq3-winners} shows that the judge's selections concentrate on the two exemplar-initialised candidates, which together win 67 of the 95 runs ($71\%$). These are the two candidates whose starting centres come from the LLM-generated codebook. Plain k-means on the raw embeddings is the only other frequent pick at 15 runs ($16\%$). Ward, averaged and rewritten k-means win eight runs combined and the two Gaussian mixtures five, so no candidate remained unpicked. Overall, k-means is by far the most selected family of candidates, with approximately $90\%$ of total picks.

\chapter{Discussion}

In this chapter, the results covered in the previous chapter will be used to attempt to answer the overarching questions of this work: whether and in what capacity the natural language reasoning of an LLM, given a one-sentence clustering criterion, can be used to evaluate, size and steer the clustering of an unlabelled corpus. These questions will be answered in the order of the four research questions as has been done in previous chapters. The rest of this chapter discusses the algorithms in the context of the literature, addresses the anomalies found in the results, proposes a modified version of the methods described and discusses the limitations that motivate the future work.

\section{Interpretation of Findings}
\label{sec:disc-interpretation}

\subsection{Research Question 1}\label{sec:disc-rq1}

The main result of research question 1 is that the label-free scores
produced by the codebook judge introduced in Section~\ref{sec:CODEBOOKJUDGE} correlate with
agreement against the benchmark labels significantly on at least 18 of
the 19 corpora for every one of the five judge LLMs, with median
correlations in the strong to very strong range, and that this
correlation largely survives when the silhouette score and Cali\'nski--Harabasz
index are controlled for. Three main aspects of the judge's algorithmic
design likely explain why this is the case.

First, the judge never scores a clustering directly. The obvious
alternative would have been to show the LLM a candidate clustering (for
example, a sample of documents per cluster) and ask it to rate the
clustering's quality on a numeric scale. That is an open-ended rating
task, and \S\ref{sec:judgebiases} documents that LLM judges on such tasks
show position bias (rating what is shown first more favourably),
verbosity bias (favouring clusters with longer or more numerous
documents) and a preference for LLM-generated over human-written text.
Instead, the judge builds a reference labelling $R$ and calculates the
AMI between $R$ and the candidate. This turns the rating task into two
tasks: listing the categories present in a sample of documents, and
classifying a single document into a fixed list. None of these tasks
prompts the LLM to compare candidates, so position bias has nothing to
act on. Additionally, neither task asks it to rate quality, so verbosity
bias has nothing to reward. The final comparison to the reference
labelling is a deterministic, chance-corrected metric that no model
biases can enter at that stage.

Second, the judge is designed specifically to measure semantic agreement
between cluster members rather than geometry, since AMI depends only on
the contingency between two labellings and never sees an embedding
coordinate or distance. This is plausibly why controlling jointly for the silhouette score and the Cali\'nski--Harabasz index only lowers the five medians in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-judgebeds} slightly (for example, from
$+0.93$ to $+0.87$ for {\small\ttfamily glm-5} and from $+0.86$ to $+0.76$ for {\small\ttfamily gpt-3.5-turbo}) and why every judge remains in the strong range.

Third, the criterion is inserted at both the codebook generation stage
and the reference-building stage, so the judge's reference is strongly
conditioned on it. The domain/intent pairs show this most clearly:
MASSIVE(D) and MASSIVE(I) are benchmark clusterings of the exact same
documents but using different criteria, and every judge still correlates
moderately to strongly with both, scoring the domain labellings between
$+0.86$ and $+0.93$ and the intent labellings between $+0.59$ and
$+0.74$. This suggests the judge is responding to the criterion sentence
rather than a fixed notion of similarity.

Interestingly, the medians only span $+0.86$ ({\small\ttfamily gpt-3.5-turbo}) to $+0.93$
({\small\ttfamily glm-5}) despite a three-year gap in release date (2023 against 2026). As such,
above a basic capability floor, LLM capacity does not seem to limit the
judge's correlation with the benchmark labelling, which practically means the
judge can be run with a cheap model.

The judge does have one systematic weakness. CLINC(I) is the only corpus
that falls in the weak band for every judge, with correlations between
$+0.16$ and $+0.30$. It is also the only corpus on which significance is
ever lost (for two of the five judges). Part of the mechanism lies in the
judge's first stage. The codebook is discovered from a sample of 300
documents. At $k = 151$ that is roughly two documents per intent, so many
intents enter the sample once or not at all. The fitted line in
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-calibration}(a) implies that a corpus of this
granularity generally receives a codebook of about 40 categories, roughly
four times coarser than the benchmark labelling. Coarseness alone does
not explain the collapse, however. The same line implies a similarly
compressed codebook for MTOP(I) at $k = 102$, yet that corpus never
approaches the weak band. Under the joint control, CLINC(I) rises to between $+0.57$
and $+0.69$ for every judge. Once the geometric signal is held fixed, the
judge's ranking still tracks AMI on this corpus. Therefore, the low raw correlation
appears to be largely the granularity suppression effect discussed above, and the
compressed codebook is at most a contributing factor. CLINC(I) does reappear
as the weakest corpus in every later experiment.

The answer to both halves of the research question is therefore yes:
given only the criterion, the judge's scores correlate strongly with
human judgement, and that correlation is more than a restatement of what
the label-free geometric metrics already measure.

\subsection{Research Question 2}

Research question 2 returned a negative result. No estimator places more than 4 of 19 corpora within $\pm 10\%$ of the benchmark $k$, and every fitted slope in Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-calibration} lies below one. Looking at the calibration plots, all estimators regress towards the middle of the granularity range, and this happens for a different reason at each end of the scale. At high $k$, the systematic weakness discussed above (\S\ref{sec:disc-rq1}) applies. An estimator that sees 300 documents simply cannot enumerate 151 categories. Most importantly, this appears to be a sampling ceiling rather than a reasoning ceiling. In the low $k$ regime, overshoot plausibly occurs because the prompt lacks more in-depth granularity constraints than the criterion. When instructed to list all evident categories, the LLM naturally captures the visible sub-topics within the broad benchmark classes, since nothing specifies a stopping criterion.

Direct is the most accurate method. It only has to state a number rather than write the categories down, so it can estimate more categories than its 300-document
sample explicitly shows. Because of this, it is also the only method that ever guesses above 200. Its fitted slope of $0.67$ is therefore also the steepest of the sample-based methods. 

The remaining methods trade accuracy against stability in different ways. Pairwise reaches the highest concordance and a lower spread by leaning on the Ward hierarchy as a scaffold. Merge convergence is by far the most stable while also being the least accurate, so a stable estimator can be stably wrong.
Simpson undershoots exactly as predicted in \S\ref{sec:RQ2_DESIGN}, and
Probe collapses to a near-constant estimate of around $13$. 

The Judge sweep (slope $0.41$) appears to be estimating the codebook's
granularity rather than the benchmark's, since the AMI against the
reference labelling is maximised when the candidate's granularity matches
that of the reference. The judge therefore carries a prior on granularity, and the right
response is to use it only where that prior affects every option equally,
which is exactly what CLEAR does by comparing all candidates at the
same $k$. Finally, the silhouette-argmax baseline matches Direct on the within-$1.5\times$ count while barely co-varying with the benchmark granularity, so matching it is no evidence of tracking $k$.

The LLM estimators do carry a real signal, with most reaching a moderate CCC, but with the exception of Pairwise, their run-to-run spread is simply too high. An estimate that can double or halve
under a fresh sample is not a usable $k$. This negative result is also
not specific to the LLM. The regression to the middle and instability
is unchanged under both replications.

The answer to research question 2 is therefore that, given only the
criterion, LLMs track the direction of granularity but cannot infer its
value. As discussed above, each method has its own failure mode, but the most apparent evidence of their overall unreliability is that no estimator places more than 4 of 19 corpora within $\pm 10\%$ of the benchmark $k$, and all but one vary by roughly their own size under a new sample. Of course, this answer is only as extensive as the eight methods that were tested. The space of possible prompts and procedures is unbounded, so potentially a better estimator than the ones covered here could always exist.


\subsection{Research Question 3}

Over the 19 benchmarks and five runs per benchmark, CLEAR reaches a mean NMI of $79.0$ and a mean ACC of $68.6$. The per-benchmark extrema run from SNIPS at the top down to CLINC(D) and FewRel at the bottom (\S\ref{sec:results-rq3-quality}). Compared against seed-paired k-means on the same embeddings, CLEAR is
ahead on 15 of 19 benchmarks in NMI (mean $+2.6$, significant at $p = 0.012$) and 13 of 19 in ACC
(mean $+3.7$). The analyses of \S\ref{sec:results-rq3-repair} and
\S\ref{sec:results-rq4-selector} show how this gain can be decomposed. Always picking the k-means candidate gives a mean NMI of $76.4$ (ACC
$64.9$). Selecting the candidate with the judge instead raises this to
$78.4$ (ACC $67.8$), and the repair step adds a further $+0.66$ (ACC
$+0.78$), arriving at CLEAR's final $79.0$ (ACC $68.6$). As such, roughly three quarters of the gain comes from selecting a better candidate and one
quarter from repairing it.

In the candidate selection, the judge picks one of the two
exemplar-initialised candidates in $71\%$ of runs. While random initialisation places centres in dense embedding regions, codebook initialisation starts at criterion-aligned category prototypes. This should help the final partition land closer to a local optimum aligned with the one-sentence criterion, which is exactly what we want. The two largest gains over simple k-means in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-uplift} are for benchmarks in which we expect this criterion-aligned start to matter most. First, on FewNerd ($+15.8$ NMI, panel (a)), the criterion is the type of a named entity (a
person, place, organisation and so on) singled out at the end of each
text, but a sentence embedding mostly encodes the sentence's overall
topic, so k-means from random starts recovers topic structure rather
than entity types. Thus, starting from the embedded prototypes of LLM-generated entities encourages the initialisation to reflect the intended criterion and thus pull the clustering towards it. Second, StackEx-S2S ($+19.4$ ACC, panel (b)) contains 121 classes. In this fine-grained regime, random initialisation often leaves benchmark classes without centres, and these classes are then often merged, whereas the codebook seeds a centre for every category the LLM found, based on the criterion.

The rewritten candidate wins only 1 of 95 runs
(Table~\ref{tab:rq3-winners}). This can plausibly be attributed to the fact that compressing each document to a 15-word phrase discards too much information the encoder needs. The averaged variants, which mix the rewrite embeddings into the raw ones instead of replacing
them altogether, are chosen on 37 total runs (30 exemplar-initialised, 4 GMM and 3 k-means in Table~\ref{tab:rq3-winners}). As such, the rewritten version is a useful supplement to the original rather than a full replacement itself. This is mostly in line with other SOTA LLM-in-the-loop algorithms in the literature such as LUMI, which averages pseudo-labels into the original vectors, and Few-Shot Clustering which concatenates keyphrases (\S\ref{sec:before}).


The judge's advantage as a selector is larger on NMI than on ACC (outperforming silhouette selection by 0.8 and 0.2 in panels (a) and (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:rq4-selector}). The judge scores using AMI, which, like NMI, does not penalise splitting a benchmark class, whereas ACC's one-to-one matching does. Therefore, the judge aligns with NMI by construction, and only indirectly with ACC. The repair gain is small relative to the candidate selection gain by design. Only 600 documents per run are reclassified and each round is kept only if
the judge score rises, so the 26 untouched runs in
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-repair-delta} are those where the judge rejected the
first round outright. Where repair actually does act, it helps in 60 runs and hurts in 9. 

It appears to help most where the text explicitly states the category, but the
embedding does not separate it. On StackOverflow ($+3.03$ NMI repair
gain in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-repair-delta}), which is clustered by
programming topic, titles about different technologies share most of
their words (e.g.\ ``how to parse a JSON string in Java'' sits next to the
same question about Python). Many titles therefore lie between
centroids, even though each explicitly names its topic. The LLM reads
the documents and can fix exactly those that the geometry placed
incorrectly. On MTOP(D), which clusters by assistant command domain and
is the one corpus where repair consistently hurts ($-1.87$ NMI in the
same figure), the opposite happens. The pre-repair clustering is already
close to the benchmark ($96.3$ NMI before repair), so the uncertain documents are often not
mistakes, but can genuinely fit two assistant domains at once. For example,
an utterance like ``remind me to call mom'' belongs to the reminder domain
under the benchmark's annotation rules, but interpreting it as a calling
request is just as defensible. The LLM thus sometimes picks the wrong
domain. Because each round is accepted by the judge rather than by the
benchmark labels, these changes are kept, causing agreement with the
benchmark to fall. The main failure case of the CLEAR algorithm is the same as in
research question 1. On CLINC(I), the judge cannot reliably order the
eight candidates (\S\ref{sec:disc-rq1}) since the codebook
covers only a fraction of the 151 intents. Consequently, the selected
candidate is no better than plain k-means and is clearly worse on ACC,
accounting for the single large ACC loss in panel (b) of
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-uplift} ($-14.1$). Repair does not improve the
performance, so it changes the score by
almost nothing ($+0.04$ NMI in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-repair-delta}). However, this weakness stays limited to a few corpora, and CLEAR
still reaches a high mean NMI and ACC over the
full suite (\S\ref{sec:results-rq3-quality}).


The answer to research question 3 is therefore that an LLM acting as a criterion-equipped partitioner and judge does align unsupervised clustering more closely with the partitions humans draw, across nearly every corpus and criterion type in the suite. Most of this alignment comes from selecting a criterion-aligned clustering rather than repairing one. This alignment fails mainly where the granularity of the human taxonomy exceeds what the judge's sampled reference can resolve.


\subsection{Research Question 4}

To remind the reader, in the unmatched configuration, CLEAR is compared to SOTA results on each benchmark under the default configuration of research question 3 ({\small\ttfamily gemini-embedding-001} as the encoder and {\small\ttfamily glm-5} as the LLM). Conversely, in the matched configuration, it adopts each benchmark's NMI record holder's encoder and LLM, so only the algorithm itself differs. In the unmatched configuration, CLEAR beats the best published result on most benchmarks on both NMI and ACC (Figure~\ref{fig:rq4-sota-both}). Matching the encoder and LLM to each record holder lowers the win counts, but a substantial share of the benchmarks are still won. This is a remarkable result since it means this work's general, criterion-driven algorithm, frozen across all 19 corpora, holds its own against a large portion of the 19 separately optimised state-of-the-art records (8 of 19 in NMI and 9 of 19 in ACC). The results of this analysis suggest that the advantage on those benchmarks is unlikely to come from stronger configurations and most likely lies in the algorithm itself. It should be noted that this default configuration is almost certainly
stronger than any configuration behind the published records, as both
{\small\ttfamily gemini-embedding-001} and {\small\ttfamily glm-5}
postdate every encoder and LLM used by the record holders
(Table~\ref{tab:rq4-sota-stacks}).

Figure~\ref{fig:rq4-factorial-delta} suggests that both ingredients matter, but in different amounts. Swapping the encoder costs roughly twice as much mean performance as swapping the LLM on NMI, and more than three times as much on ACC. This makes sense when analysing CLEAR's design. The encoder alone likely does most of the heavy lifting in shaping the distribution of text in the embedding space. An LLM can enhance this positioning (via the averaged or rewritten embeddings), but the encoder largely dictates the primary structure. Everything else the LLM contributes (the codebook, exemplar starts, the judge's selection, and repair rounds) builds on top of this base representation to guide or refine the clustering. As such, a stronger encoder should improve the whole clustering pipeline, and {\small\ttfamily gemini-embedding-001} (Jul 2025) is stronger than many SOTA encoders in the literature, such as the {\small\ttfamily instructor-large} (Dec 2022) encoder used in 13 out of 19 benchmarks. As expected, CLINC(I) is again the weakest benchmark in the matched configuration, for the same reason as in the previous research questions. The judge's sampled reference cannot resolve its 151-intent granularity (\S\ref{sec:disc-rq1}).

The answer to research question 4 is therefore a qualified yes. With the encoder and language model held equal, the single, general CLEAR algorithm matches or exceeds the published LLM-in-the-loop record on a fair share of the suite, falls behind mainly where the record holder is a specialist for that corpus's criterion, and gains a distinct overall advantage as soon as its ingredients are allowed to be modern ones.

\section{Conclusion}

This thesis asked whether the natural language reasoning of an LLM,
given access to a one-sentence clustering criterion, can be used
to evaluate, infer the granularity of, and generate the clustering of an unlabelled corpus. Each
of the four research questions answered one part of that question, and
together they draw a consistent picture of where criterion-conditioned
LLM reasoning helps, where it does not, and why.

For evaluation, the answer is yes. The codebook judge of
\S\ref{sec:CODEBOOKJUDGE} converts a one-sentence criterion and the corpus into a
reference labelling of that corpus and scores any candidate clustering against it
without ever seeing a benchmark label. Across the full suite of corpora
and criterion types, its scores correlate significantly with agreement
against the benchmark labelling on nearly every corpus for every one of the
five LLMs tested, with median correlations in the strong to very
strong range. The correlation mainly survives joint control for the
silhouette score and the Cali\'nski--Harabasz index. The judge is
therefore not a restatement of geometry. It does fail in one predictable regime: corpora whose
granularity exceeds what its sampled discovery stage can resolve (e.g.\ CLINC(I)). 


For granularity estimation, the answer is a clear no. None of the eight granularity estimators, whether they rely on enumeration, direct estimation, vote aggregation or hybrid
geometric designs, recovers the benchmark granularity with useful
accuracy.




Every calibration slope lies below one, and most estimates
vary by roughly their own size under different random seeds. Replicating the entire experiment with two further LLMs reproduced the
same negative result, so the failure seems to stem not from the capacity of
any single model but from the task itself. The practical consequence is therefore that granularity is an analyst's choice and must
remain a user-controlled input if the CLEAR algorithm is to be used in production.




For actual clustering, the answer is also yes but within some defined limits. CLEAR leverages the fact that the validated judge algorithm from research question 1 correlates significantly with human evaluation to perform clustering itself. It does this by turning the judge into an active component that selects among eight candidate clusterings and performs an additional bounded repair step. This lets the clustering algorithm be tailored to the corpus by choosing the base clustering algorithm that produces the highest human-aligned score. It beats seed-paired k-means on the same embeddings on most benchmarks on both metrics. As expected, most of that gain comes from the candidate selection process and the remainder from repairing it, where an LLM conditioned on the criterion is used in both. 

Against the published state of the art, CLEAR wins most benchmarks in its own modern configuration, and still holds a substantial share of the records when compared on an LLM- and encoder-equal basis. The decomposition of LLM and encoder contribution attributes roughly twice as much of the effect of configuration to the encoder as to the LLM. As such, the results indicate that the choice of encoder, as well as the LLM, substantially affects CLEAR's performance. 

One important caveat applies to every result in this thesis.
Whenever results show the judge's agreement with human
judgement, an estimator's recovery of granularity, or a
clustering's quality against human labels, it is important to remember that the quantities 
computed are measuring agreement with one specific benchmark labelling, produced
by one or more particular annotators under a given annotation scheme. As such, results that show agreement with human clustering can only be said to show alignment with the annotators who built these benchmarks. The generalisation to other
human clusterings is therefore strictly an assumption rather than a finding.


\section{Contributions}

The contributions in this work can be summarised in three main points:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A new label-free, LLM-powered clustering evaluation method. In the first large-scale validation of its kind, its scores are shown to correlate strongly
    with benchmark labels across corpora, criterion types and judge LLMs,
    beyond what geometric metrics already measure.
    \item A systematic study showing that the granularity of a human
    taxonomy cannot be reliably inferred from the criterion and the
    unlabelled corpus alone, at least not by any of the
    estimation methods tested here, across three different LLMs.
     \item CLEAR, a general clustering algorithm that takes as input only the corpus, the criterion and a target $k$. It beats the published state of the art on most benchmarks in its own configuration, and still wins a substantial share when matched to each record holder's own encoder and LLM configuration. As of the time of writing, this work's proposed clustering algorithm is therefore fully competitive with state-of-the-art LLM-in-the-loop text clustering algorithms.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Work}

As identified in \S\ref{sec:disc-rq1}, the granularity ceiling is the biggest weakness of both the codebook judge and the CLEAR algorithm. Most weaknesses that recur across the four research questions trace back to the judge's discovery-stage reading of a 300-document sample, which prevents it from resolving taxonomies with 100 or more categories, such as CLINC(I). This 300-document choice was made to trade off context-window fit against sampling breadth. A promising next step could be a hierarchical discovery stage. The LLM would
first build a coarse codebook from the sample and would then be prompted to split
each category into subcategories. This would multiply the granularity the judge can resolve
without enlarging the actual sample.


In the configuration ablation of research question 4, we found that the encoder contributed roughly twice as much to the overall performance of the CLEAR algorithm as the LLM. Enhancing the encoder is therefore a promising direction for future research. CLEAR currently treats the encoder as frozen. Fine-tuning
the encoder on judge-derived signals, for example, criterion-conditioned triplets in the style of ClusterLLM but labelled by the codebook judge rather than by benchmark labels,
would allow the representation to adapt to the criterion and might therefore significantly boost performance. A CLEAR variant fine-tuned this way could then also be compared directly
against other state-of-the-art encoder fine-tuning methods. This was deliberately left out of the scope in this work. 


Finally, the caveat of \S\ref{sec:disc-interpretation} could be investigated empirically. All results here measure agreement with one benchmark labelling per corpus and not human labelling in general. A large-scale human study in which fresh annotators cluster corpora under stated criteria would
test whether the judge tracks human judgement in general rather than specific benchmarks. This would, in a certain way, generalise the result.
